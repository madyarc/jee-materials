Superposition, Standing Waves and Beats

When two waves meet, the medium simply adds their displacements. That one rule explains why pulses pass through each other, why a pulse flips over at a fixed end, why a guitar string or a flute can only sound certain notes, and why two nearly equal notes throb. The last section adds the Doppler effect: what motion does to the note you hear.

1 · Two pulses pass through each other

Two pulses run toward each other on a long string. Scrub the time, or play. Dashed: each pulse on its own. Solid: the actual string, their sum.

pulse moving rightpulse moving leftstring = sum

Principle of superposition: y = y₁ + y₂. Each pulse moves as if the other were not there, and comes out unchanged. This holds for small displacements, which is why it is a model, not a law for every medium.

Two waves in the same direction: interference

Two waves of equal amplitude a and the same frequency travel the same way, one shifted by a phase difference φ. Their sum is again a wave of the same λ and ν, but its amplitude depends on φ.

Resultant amplitude 2a cos(φ/2): 2a in phase (φ = 0, 2π…, constructive), 0 when φ = π (destructive).

2 · Reflection: fixed end and free end

A pulse reaches the end of the string. At a fixed end (tied to a wall) the end cannot move. At a free end (a light ring sliding on a smooth rod) nothing holds it sideways. Predict which one sends the pulse back upside down, then play.

The trick: imagine the string continues past the end, with a mirror-image pulse coming the other way. Fixed end: the image is upside down, so the two always cancel at the wall (y = 0 there). Free end: the image is upright, so at the end they add and the ring swings to twice the height. Rigid boundary → phase change π; free boundary → no phase change. The open end of an organ pipe behaves like the free end.

At a join between two different strings (or two media) part of the pulse goes on and part comes back. Going obliquely into a new medium, the transmitted wave bends: refraction, with Snell’s law.

3 · Two opposite waves make a standing wave

A wave going right and its equal going left (for example, a wave and its reflection). Their sum does not travel at all: some points never move (nodes), others swing the most (antinodes).

a sin(kx − ωt), going righta sin(kx + ωt), going leftsumenvelope ±2a sin kx

y = a sin(kx − ωt) + a sin(kx + ωt) = 2a sin kx · cos ωt. Position and time now sit in separate factors. Each point oscillates with the same ω, with its own fixed amplitude 2a|sin kx|.

Nodes where sin kx = 0: x = 0, λ/2, λ, … Antinodes where |sin kx| = 1: x = λ/4, 3λ/4, … Node to node: λ/2. Node to the next antinode: λ/4.

All points between two neighbouring nodes move in step (same phase), reaching their extremes together; the next loop moves the opposite way. In a travelling wave it is the other way round: same amplitude everywhere, phase changing along x.

Energy does not flow past a node (nothing there moves), so a standing wave stores energy rather than carrying it.

4 · A string fixed at both ends

Both ends must be nodes. So a whole number of half-wavelengths has to fit on the string: L = nλ/2. Only these wavelengths, and so only these frequencies, survive. Step through the modes.

Allowed frequencies

λₙ = 2L/n and νₙ = n·v/(2L) = (n/2L)√(T/µ), n = 1, 2, 3, … The lowest, n = 1, is the fundamental (first harmonic); every mode is a whole-number multiple of it.

Shorter, tighter or lighter strings sound higher: that is how a sitar player’s finger, the tuning peg and the thinner strings work (the sonometer checks ν ∝ 1/L at fixed tension). A real plucked string vibrates in many modes at once; where you pluck or bow decides which are strong (plucking at the middle, an antinode of odd modes and a node of even ones, gives almost no even harmonics). That mixture is why a violin and a sitar playing the same note sound different. A tabla skin has its own modes, set by its fixed rim.

5 · Air columns: closed and open pipes

Sound in a pipe is longitudinal, but the rule is the same. The air at a closed end cannot move: a displacement node. At an open end the air moves freely: a displacement antinode. Count what fits.

Allowed frequencies, same length L

The vertical lines are layers of air, moving along the pipe. The shaded lobes draw how far each layer swings, turned sideways so you can see it (schematic). Where layers swing the most, the pressure hardly changes; where they cannot move, neighbours squeeze against them and the pressure swings most. So a displacement node is a pressure antinode, and the other way round.

rule of thumb The antinode actually sits a little outside an open end. Add an end correction of about 0.6 × (pipe radius) per open end to L. Exam questions say whether to include it.

6 · Beats

Two notes of nearly the same frequency. They drift in and out of step: in step they add, half a beat later they cancel. You hear one tone whose loudness throbs.

ν₁ν₂sumenvelope
Sound is off until you press Listen. Both notes are raised by 400 Hz so you can hear them; the beat rate is unchanged.

s = a cos ω₁t + a cos ω₂t = [2a cos((ω₁ − ω₂)t/2)] · cos((ω₁ + ω₂)t/2). A fast oscillation at the average frequency inside a slow envelope. Loudness peaks whenever the envelope reaches +2a or −2a, so there are |ν₁ − ν₂| beats per second, twice the envelope’s own frequency.

Musicians tune by ear this way: adjust until the beats slow down and vanish. Beats are easy to hear only when the difference is small (a few per second); a larger difference sounds rough, then as two notes.

7 · Doppler effect JEE Advanced

A siren sounds higher coming toward you and lower going away. The source does not change its note; the motion changes how closely the wavefronts are packed, and how fast you run into them. Each circle below is one wavefront, drawn round the point where the source was when it sent it.

source Sobserver Ovelocities

Positive = moving toward the other one.

Read the signs off the picture: approaching → higher. A source moving toward O crowds its wavefronts ahead of it: λ shrinks to (v − vs)/ν, so subtract vs in the denominator (add it when moving away). An observer moving toward S runs into the wavefronts faster, at v + vo relative to them, so add vo in the numerator (subtract when moving away). Together: ν′ = ν (v ± vo)/(v ∓ vs), upper signs for approaching. The wave itself still travels at v through the air whatever the source does; only λ and the rate of meeting change.
