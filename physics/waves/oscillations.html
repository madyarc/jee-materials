Oscillations and SHM

A swing, a guitar string, a mass on a spring, the atoms in a solid: each one moves to and fro about a point where it could rest. The simplest such motion, simple harmonic motion, is just the shadow of a point going round a circle at a steady speed. This page builds everything from that one picture: displacement, velocity, acceleration, the force law, energy, the spring and the pendulum.

1 · Periodic, oscillatory, simple harmonic

Watch each motion and its graph, then sort it into the right box. A motion that repeats itself at regular intervals is periodic. If it goes to and fro about an equilibrium point inside its path, it is also oscillatory. If the force pulling it back is proportional to its distance from that point, it is simple harmonic.

Every oscillation is periodic, but not every periodic motion is an oscillation. The number of repeats per second is the frequency ν, measured in hertz (1 Hz = one repeat per second), and the time for one repeat is the period T = 1/ν. Real oscillations die out because of friction and air drag (damping); sections 1–9 leave damping out, and section 10 puts it back in. Whether it is called an oscillation or a vibration is only a matter of how fast it is. And the thing that oscillates need not be a position: the angle of a pendulum, the voltage across a capacitor in an AC circuit or the pressure in a sound wave can all go to and fro in the same way.

2 · SHM is the shadow of circular motion

The point P goes round the circle anticlockwise at a steady angular speed ω. Light from above casts its shadow P′ on the horizontal diameter. The shadow just goes to and fro, and the strip below records where it was. Like the paper in a chart recorder, the strip scrolls downward, so the newest point is at the top and earlier moments lie further down.

The shadow’s position is x = A cos(ωt + φ). The amplitude A is the radius of the circle. The phase ωt + φ is the angle of P from the +x axis right now; its starting value φ, the phase constant, says where P was at t = 0 (hollow dot). One trip round takes T = 2π/ω.

Turn on the vertical shadow: y = A sin(ωt + φ). It is the same SHM a quarter-turn (π/2) behind. Turn on velocity and acceleration: the shadow’s velocity and acceleration are just the horizontal parts of P’s arrows, which is all of section 3.

The circle is only a way to see the motion. A block on a spring has no point going round anywhere, and the force on it is not the centripetal force of circular motion. It moves exactly like this shadow, though.

3 · Displacement, velocity, acceleration

The same motion as above (same A, ω and φ), drawn as three graphs against time. Each is scaled to its own peak, so you can compare their timing. The dot on the track is the moving body, with its velocity and acceleration arrows.

Shares its clock with section 2.

v leads x by a quarter period (π/2): the velocity peaks T/4 before the displacement does, while the body is racing through the centre. At the ends, where x is largest, v is zero. a is x upside down (phase difference π): a = −ω²x.

Peak sizes: vmax = ωA at the centre, amax = ω²A at the ends. Between them, the speed at displacement x is v = ω√(A² − x²): the shadow’s speed is the horizontal part of P’s speed ωA.

4 · The restoring force: F = −kx

Drag the block to stretch or squash the spring, then let go. The spring always pulls or pushes it back toward x = 0, harder the further away it is. That is the whole recipe for SHM.

Frictionless floor; the spring is massless.

Hooke’s law: F = −kx. The minus sign is the arrow pointing back to the centre. Newton’s second law then gives a = −(k/m)x, which is a = −ω²x from section 3 with ω² = k/m. So any force that is proportional to displacement and points back to the equilibrium point gives SHM, with ω = √(k/m). A straight line through the origin with a negative slope on this graph is the signature. Real springs (and most forces) add small x² or x³ terms when stretched far; those are non-linear oscillators and are not exactly SHM.

T = 2π√(m/k) contains no amplitude. Pulled twice as far, B feels twice the force, so it has twice the acceleration all the way and covers twice the distance in the same time. The period is also independent of the phase constant and of the energy. (A pendulum keeps this property only for small swings: section 7.)

6 · Energy sloshes between kinetic and potential

A 1 kg block on a spring with k = 50 N m⁻¹. Above each position x, the bar shows how the total energy is split: the stored spring energy (potential) up to the bowl-shaped curve, and the rest kinetic.

Stop it at:

Against time

U = ½kx² is the parabola; it depends only on where the block is. The total E = ½kA² is the flat line, the same at every x and every moment (no friction). Kinetic energy K = E − U is the gap between them: all kinetic at the centre, all potential at the ends. Double the amplitude and E becomes four times as large.

On the time graph, K and U each repeat every T/2, twice per oscillation, because the speed and the stretch reach their peaks twice. Averaged over a cycle, each is exactly half the total energy.

7 · The simple pendulum, and where the approximation breaks

A point bob on a light string. Drag the bob out and let go. The solid pendulum moves exactly as Newton’s laws say. The dashed ghost follows the small-angle model, which replaces sin θ by θ to make the motion SHM. Small swings: they stay together. Big swings: watch the ghost get ahead.

The string’s pull and the part mg cos θ act along the string and only keep the bob on its circle. The part along the arc, mg sin θ, pulls it back. Its torque about the support is −mgL sin θ = Iα with I = mL², so α = −(g/L) sin θ. For small θ, sin θ ≈ θ (in radians) and α = −(g/L)θ: SHM with ω² = g/L, so T = 2π√(L/g). The mass has cancelled.

Where it breaks rule of thumb: at 20°, sin θ is 2% smaller than θ but the period is only 0.8% longer than 2π√(L/g); at 60°, the period is 7% longer and at 90°, 18%. The real restoring force mg sin θ is weaker than mgθ, so the real pendulum is always slower. The true period here is computed exactly from Newton’s law, which goes beyond the syllabus; you only need to know that it grows with amplitude. For a rigid body swinging about a pivot, the same steps give T = 2π√(I/mgd), with d the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass (see moment of inertia).

10 · Damped and forced oscillations, resonance JEE Advanced

Real oscillators lose energy to friction and drag, so their swings shrink. Push them in rhythm, though, and they can swing harder and harder. Both effects are shown here with the model used in textbooks: a drag force −bv proportional to the velocity, added to the spring force −kx.

Damped: pull it out, let go, watch it die out

displacement xenvelope ±A e−bt/2m

Light damping: x = A e−bt/2m cos(ω′t + φ), with ω′ = √(k/m − b²/4m²), a little slower than ω₀ = √(k/m). The amplitude shrinks by the same fraction in every equal time interval: that is exponential decay. Energy ∝ (amplitude)², so it decays twice as fast, as e−bt/m. The oscillation stops at the critical value bc = 2√(km), where the body returns fastest without overshooting.

Forced: push it in rhythm at ωd

Steady state, after the start-up motion has died away.

Sweep ωd: the steady-state amplitude peaks when you drive close to the natural frequency ω₀. That is resonance. Lower the damping and the peak gets taller and narrower (faint curves: other damping values). With no damping at all the model’s amplitude would grow without limit; damping is what caps it.

A = (F₀/m) ÷ √[(ω₀² − ωd²)² + (bωd/m)²]. The body always oscillates at the driving frequency, not its own. Watch the timing too: driven slowly it moves in step with the push; at resonance it lags a quarter cycle, so the push is always along its velocity and keeps feeding in energy; driven fast it moves opposite to the push.

Real examples qualitative: a swing pushed once per swing goes higher and higher; London’s Millennium Footbridge swayed so much when crowds first walked on it, their steps falling into rhythm with its sway, that it was closed for about two years; a radio is tuned by bringing its circuit’s natural frequency to the station’s. Car shock absorbers are made close to critically damped, so the car settles after a bump without bouncing.

Models used: point masses; massless springs that obey Hooke’s law exactly; massless, inextensible strings; frictionless floors; no air drag or other damping in sections 1–9, so nothing ever stops there. Section 10 uses the standard model of damping, a drag force −bv proportional to the velocity, and a driving force F₀ cos ωdt; for the driven oscillator it shows only the steady state reached once the start-up motion has died away. The small-angle model (sin θ ≈ θ) is labelled where it is used. g = 9.8 m s⁻² on Earth and 1.62 m s⁻² on the Moon, unless a question says otherwise. Drawings of springs and pendulum lengths are schematic, not to scale; section 8 runs in slow or fast motion so that A always takes 2 s per full oscillation.
