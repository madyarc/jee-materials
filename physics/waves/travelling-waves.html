Travelling Waves

A wave is a pattern that moves, not stuff that moves. Every bit of the string or the air only wobbles about its own place, slightly out of step with its neighbour, and that delay makes the shape travel. This page builds the wave equation, the wave speed and the speed of sound from that one picture.

1 · The pattern moves, the particles don’t

A row of particles joined by springs (a model of a string, or of layers of air). One end is shaken steadily. Watch the green particle: does it travel with the wave?

one particle and its range of motionwave crest, moving right

Transverse waves need a medium that resists being sheared (a solid, or a stretched string). Every medium resists being squashed, so longitudinal waves travel in solids, liquids and gases; air carries only longitudinal waves. Water-surface waves are a mixture: each bit of water moves up-and-down and back-and-forth. Light and radio waves need no medium at all (they are electromagnetic, Class 12); everything on this page is a mechanical wave.

2 · One equation, two pictures

The same wave can be looked at in two ways. Freeze time and look along the string: a snapshot, y against x. Or fix one particle and watch it: its history, y against t. Drag the green particle P along the string.

History: particle P over time

particle P× a crest (fixed phase)particle velocity

Snapshot: points one wavelength λ apart are in the same phase, so k = 2π/λ. History: P repeats after one period T, so ω = 2π/T and ν = 1/T. In one period the crest (×) moves exactly one wavelength, so v = λ/T = νλ = ω/k. Every particle has the same amplitude and period; they differ only in phase, P lagging the particle at x = 0 by kx (for a wave moving in +x). The phase φ just slides the whole pattern. (Writing y = A sin(kx − ωt) + B cos(kx − ωt) is the same wave, with a = √(A² + B²).)

Any shape f(x − vt) travels to +x at speed v, sinusoidal or not; f(x + vt) travels to −x. For a longitudinal wave the same equation gives s(x, t), the displacement along x.

Predict: is the marked particle moving up or down?

The arrow shows which way the wave travels. Trick: the wave shape shifts a little that way; ask whether the curve under the dot rises or falls.

Pick an answer.

Shift the shape by a tiny dx = v·dt: the particle’s new height is the old curve’s height a little behind it. So particle velocity = −v × (slope of the snapshot) for a wave moving +x, and +v × slope for −x. At a crest or trough the slope is zero: momentarily at rest. On the steepest part (crossing the axis) it moves fastest, at aω.

3 · What sets the speed: the string

Tension pulls a displaced bit of string back; its mass resists being moved. More pull, faster wave; more mass per metre, slower. The source decides how often it shakes (ν). The string decides the speed, and the wavelength follows: λ = v/ν.

Shown slowed down 400×.

v = √(T/µ). Dimensions alone give √(T/µ) times an unknown number; the full derivation from Newton’s laws shows that number is 1. The speed depends only on the string, not on ν or λ: change ν and only λ changes.

4 · The speed of sound: Newton’s guess and Laplace’s fix

For sound the restoring “spring” is the air’s resistance to squashing, its bulk modulus B = −ΔP/(ΔV/V); the inertia is its density ρ. So v = √(B/ρ). The question is: how stiff is air when sound squeezes it?

Squeezing a parcel of air

Predicted speed

Pressure cancels: at fixed temperature ρ is proportional to P (ideal gas, ρ = PM/RT), so P/ρ = RT/M and v = √(γRT/M). Speed rises as √T. Moist air is slightly faster: water vapour (M = 18 g/mol) is lighter than dry air, so ρ drops (qualitative; γ barely changes).

Liquids and solids are far harder to squash, so B rises much more than ρ and sound is faster in them. In a thin solid bar the stiffness that counts is Young’s modulus: v = √(Y/ρ).
