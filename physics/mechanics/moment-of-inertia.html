Moment of Inertia

Moment of inertia is how hard a body is to spin about a given axis. It depends on one thing only: how far each bit of mass sits from that axis. Every bit counts by the square of its distance. Pick a body, move the axis, and see the number follow the picture.

The more mass sits near the rim, the larger I. Each bit counts by r⊥², so the bands near the rim count far more than their share of mass (compare the two bar colours above).

Radius of gyration k: squeeze all the mass onto one thin ring of radius k and you get the same I. So I = Mk², and k is a kind of "average distance" of the mass from the axis, weighted by squares. It is not the distance of the centre of mass from the axis.

2 · Move the axis: parallel-axis theorem

Drag the axis (⊙, perpendicular to the page) anywhere. The new axis is parallel to the one through the centre of mass (CM). Moving it a distance d adds exactly Md², whatever the body.

Watch for: the theorem must start from the axis through the CM. To go from an axis A (not through the CM) to a parallel axis B, step through the CM: IB = IA − MdA² + MdB². Every axis on the faint circle has the same I, and the smallest I of the whole family is at the CM.

3 · Flat bodies: perpendicular-axis theorem

For a flat sheet (a lamina), take two perpendicular axes x′ and y′ in its plane and a third axis z through the same point, sticking out of the page. Turn the x′y′ pair or change the shape: Ix′ and Iy′ trade off, but their sum never moves. Drag the small mass dm to see why.

For the small mass: its distance to z is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are its distances to x′ and y′, so r² = x′² + y′². This works only because all three distances lie in the plane of the sheet.

Only for laminae. For a cube, every axis through the centre has I = Ma²/6, so Ix + Iy = Ma²/3 ≠ Iz. A bit of mass at height h off the x–y plane gets h² added to its squared distance from x and from y, but not from z. So for a solid body Ix + Iy = Iz + 2Σ m h², which is more than Iz.
