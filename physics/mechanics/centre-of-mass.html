Centre of Mass

A body made of many particles still has one special point that moves as simply as a single particle would: the centre of mass (CM). Find it by dragging masses around, by symmetry, by splitting a body into parts. Then watch it in action: a shell that explodes in mid-air, a person walking on a boat, two stars circling each other.

1 · A weighted average of positions

Drag the masses (and the origin O). Change a mass with − and +. The CM marker ⊕ moves at once. Watch the ticks on the axes: the CM’s x is an average of the masses’ x values in which a heavier mass counts more.

In symbols, X = Σmixi/M and Y = Σmiyi/M, or in one line R = Σmiri/M. Move O and the numbers change, but ⊕ stays on the same spot among the masses: the CM belongs to the body, not to the axes. Put O at the CM and Σmiri becomes zero.

2 · Bodies: symmetry first, then parts

A solid body is a crowd of tiny masses, so R = (1/M)∫r dm. You rarely need the integral. If every bit of mass has a twin on the opposite side of a line, the CM lies on that line. Where a body has no full symmetry, slide the ? marker to your guess, then reveal.

3 · The CM ignores what happens inside

Throw a baton so it tumbles, or fire a shell that explodes mid-air. The pieces do complicated things. The CM (⊕) does not care: the only external force is gravity, so it keeps to the same parabola it would have followed anyway (dashed). Scrub the time or press Play.

Momentum now

Each piece’s momentum mivi, added head to tail, ends exactly where M·vcm (black) ends.

Kinetic energy now

The internal forces (the explosion, the rod holding the baton together) come in equal and opposite pairs, so they cancel in the sum: M·acm = Fext. Summing mivi the same way gives the total momentum P = M·vcm, and dP/dt = Fext. Kinetic energy splits too: K = ½Mvcm² + K′, where K′ is the energy of motion relative to the CM (spin of the baton, pieces flying apart). The explosion adds to K′ from its chemical energy; it cannot touch ½Mvcm².

4 · Walking on a boat

A person stands at one end of a boat on still water. Drag them (or use the slider) to walk to the other end. Before you do: will the boat move, and how far?

Feet push the boat back; the boat pushes the feet forward. Both forces are internal to the person + boat system, and still water gives no horizontal force (in this model). So the CM, at rest at the start, stays at rest: m·(person’s shift) = M·(boat’s shift), in opposite directions. Had the boat been drifting at a steady speed, the CM would keep that same speed, whatever the person did on board.

5 · Ride along with the CM

With no external force, the CM moves in a straight line at steady speed. So watch from a frame that moves with it: tangled paths become simple.

This is a standard trick in problems: describe the motion of the CM (simple, set by external forces) and the motion about the CM (set by internal forces) separately, then add them.

6 · Centre of gravity: hang it up

Hang a flat card from a pin through one hole. It swings and settles. Hang it from another hole. The plumb lines (dashed) are drawn on the card itself. Predict: where do they meet?

Hang from:

Pick a hole to hang the card from.

Each bit of the card has weight mig. The centre of gravity (CG) is the point about which all these weights give zero total torque, so the whole weight acts there. The card stops swinging only when that point is straight below the pin, because then the weight has no lever arm about the pin. Balance the card on a pencil tip at the CG and it stays level.

When g is the same at every bit of the body, the g cancels out of Σmiri × g = 0, leaving Σmiri = 0: the CG is the CM. For a body so large that g changes from one end to the other (a mountain, a very tall tower), the CG and CM are slightly apart. The CM depends only on how mass is spread; it would exist with no gravity at all.

Torque, lever arm and the conditions for balance are built up in Torque and Angular Momentum.
