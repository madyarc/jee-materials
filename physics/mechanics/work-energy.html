Work and Energy

A force does work when it pushes something along. Work is how energy gets moved in and out of motion (kinetic energy) and in and out of storage (potential energy). Drag the arrows, move the ball on the energy landscape, and watch the bars: energy only ever changes form or place, and work is the way it does so.

1 · Work: only the part of the force along the motion counts

The block moves a distance d to the right. Drag the tip of the orange force arrow round. Its “shadow” on the direction of motion, F cos θ, is the part that does work.

W = F d cos θ = F·d, the scalar (dot) product. In components it is Fxdx + Fydy: here d is along x, so only Fx = F cos θ survives. Work is a scalar with a sign, measured in joules (1 J = 1 N m, dimensions [ML²T⁻²]).

No work is done when there is no displacement (pushing a wall, holding a load still), no force (a puck gliding on ice), or when force and motion are perpendicular (carrying a bag across a level floor; gravity on a body in a circular orbit).

2 · A changing force: work is the area under F–x

Chop the path into thin strips. In each strip the force is nearly constant, so its work is F Δx, a thin rectangle. Add the rectangles; as they get thinner the sum becomes the area under the curve. Area below the axis counts as negative work.

strips:

Work–energy theorem: the work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy, Wnet = Kf − Ki, with K = ½mv². The teal bar is K: it grows where the net area grows and shrinks where it is negative.

The theorem is Newton’s second law added up along the path (dK = F dx). It is a scalar statement, so it says nothing about direction, and nothing about how long the motion took. Its strength: you can find the work of a force you don’t know, such as air resistance, from the change in K.

3 · Same climb, different paths: conservative or not

Slide a 1 kg block slowly from A to B (3 m higher) along different tracks. Watch two running totals: the work done by gravity and the work done by friction. Predict first: which one ends up the same on every path?

work by gravitywork by friction (dashed)faint: the other paths

Gravity is conservative. Its work depends only on where you start and finish: −mgh = −29.4 J for every path to B, and zero for any round trip. That is what lets us store it as potential energy: ΔU = −Wgravity, so U = mgh (taking g constant, fine for h much less than the Earth’s radius).

Friction is not. It always opposes the motion, so its work keeps piling up with distance and never cancels on the way back. No potential energy can be defined for it. simplified model friction here has a fixed size of 2 N along the path.

Three equivalent tests for a conservative force: it comes from a potential, F = −dU/dx; its work depends only on the end points; its work round any closed path is zero. Only changes in U matter, so where you put U = 0 is your choice; keep it fixed through a problem.

4 · The potential-energy landscape

The curve is U(x). The dashed line is the total energy E, which stays fixed. Where the ball sits, the gap between the line and the curve is its kinetic energy. Drag the ball to place it at rest anywhere (E then equals U there), or raise the energy line with its handle on the right to give it a push. Then release it.

U(x)kinetic energy, K = E − Uforce F = −dU/dxvelocity▲ turning point

Read the slope as a force. F = −dU/dx: the force always points downhill on the curve, and is bigger where the curve is steeper. The lower graph is that slope, flipped.

Turning points are where E = U: there K = 0, the ball stops and turns back. It can never enter the shaded parts, where U > E would need negative K. Equilibrium is where the slope is zero (F = 0): at a valley bottom it is stable (nudge it, it comes back), on a hilltop unstable (it rolls away), on a flat stretch neutral (it just stays wherever you leave it).

5 · The spring: a force that grows as you pull

Drag the block to stretch or compress the spring. The spring always pulls back towards x = 0 (Hooke’s law, F = −kx). The work you do is the shaded area under your force, a triangle, and it is stored in the spring.

spring force, −kxyour hand, +kx (dashed)work you do = energy storedextension x

From 0 to x the triangle has base x and height kx, so the work stored is U = ½kx². The spring itself does the opposite work, −½kx². Starting from x₁ instead, the area is a trapezium: W = ½k(x² − x₁²).

Stretch and come back to where you started: the two areas cancel and the spring’s total work is zero. So the spring force is conservative, and ½kx² is its potential energy (zero at the natural length). A large k is a stiff spring; k is in N m⁻¹. ideal model massless spring obeying Hooke’s law exactly.

6 · Energy bars: watch it change form

Pick a system and let it go. Kinetic and potential energy trade back and forth while their sum stays level. Switch on friction: the sum now leaks away as heat, but the grand total (including heat) still does not change.

Mechanical energy K + U is conserved only when the forces doing work are conservative. With friction, its change equals the work of the non-conservative forces: Ef − Ei = Wnc (negative, so E falls). The tension in a pendulum string does no work: it is always perpendicular to the motion.

7 · Round a vertical circle

Give the bob a speed u at the bottom and let it climb. Energy conservation fixes its speed at every height. What it is attached to then decides whether it gets round: a string can only pull, a rod (or a bead threaded on a ring) can pull or push. Predict first: for a string, is u = 2√(gL), just enough to reach the top height, enough?

L = 1.0 m, m = 1 kg, slowed down 2×
forces: weight mg, and the pull (or push) along the string, rod or ringvelocitypath it will follow

Measure θ from the bottom. Energy: v² = u² − 2gL(1 − cos θ). Toward the centre, the pull T and the weight’s component must give the centripetal force: T − mg cos θ = mv²/L, so T = mv²/L + mg cos θ = m(u²/L − 2g + 3g cos θ). T is largest at the bottom and smallest at the top, and Tbottom − Ttop = 6mg whatever u is.

String: needs T ≥ 0 at the top, so u ≥ √(5gL) (then vtop ≥ √(gL)). For √(2gL) < u < √(5gL), T reaches zero above the centre, at cos θ = (2gL − u²)/(3gL): the string goes slack and the bob leaves on a parabola. For u ≤ √(2gL) it never rises above the centre and just swings. Rod or bead: it can push, so the bob only has to reach the top: u ≥ 2√(gL) (vtop may be 0). Below that it swings back.

8 · Power: how fast the work is done

Two lifts raise the same load through the same 12 m, so they do the same work against gravity. Set their speeds and run them. On the work–time graph, the steeper line is the more powerful lift.

Average power P = W/t. Instantaneous power P = dW/dt = F·v. A lift at constant speed must pull with F = mg, so P = mgv: double the speed, double the power, same total work. simplified model no friction in the shaft.

Units: 1 watt = 1 J s⁻¹; 1 horsepower (hp) = 746 W. The kilowatt hour on an electricity bill is energy, not power: 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. In atomic physics you will meet the electronvolt, 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
