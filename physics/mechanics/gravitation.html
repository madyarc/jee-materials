Gravitation

Tycho Brahe spent his life measuring where the planets were. His assistant Kepler found three patterns in those numbers, and Newton showed that one force explains all three: every mass pulls every other mass, and the pull falls off as 1/r². This page builds that law from the orbits, then uses it on the Earth: how strong g is, how it changes above and below the surface, and where the familiar mgh comes from.

Satellites, escape speed and orbital energy are on the next page: Orbits, Escape and Satellites.

1 · Ellipses and equal areas

Every planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus (law of orbits). The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (law of areas). Each shaded wedge below is swept in the same time, one twelfth of a year. Raise the eccentricity and watch the wedges near the Sun get short and fat while the far ones get long and thin.

The planet speeds up near the Sun (perihelion, P) and slows far away (aphelion, A). Gravity always points at the Sun, so it has no turning effect about the Sun and the planet’s angular momentum L stays constant. The area swept per second is L/2m, the same everywhere on the orbit. So rPvP = rAvA, and vP/vA = (1 + e)/(1 − e).

Tick “string to both foci”: the two distances from the planet always add up to the same length, 2a (the long axis). That is how you draw an ellipse with a loop of string and two pins. At e = 0 the foci merge and the ellipse is a circle. Real planets are close to that: only Mercury and Mars (of the eight) are visibly off-centre. The e = 0.5 orbit is exaggerated to make the effect easy to see.

2 · The law of periods: T² ∝ a³

Farther planets take longer to go round, and not just because their path is longer: they also move more slowly. Plot each planet’s period T against the size of its orbit a (the semi-major axis). On ordinary axes you get a curve; on log–log axes, every planet falls on one straight line of slope 3/2.

For a circular orbit, gravity supplies the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, and v = 2πr/T. Eliminate v and T² = (4π²/GM) r³. The constant depends only on the Sun’s mass M, so it is the same for every planet. In years and AU (1 AU = Earth’s orbit) it is 1: T² = a³. For an ellipse, r becomes the semi-major axis a.

T² ÷ a³ for each planet

Real planet data (mean distance from the Sun and orbital period). Every ratio is within about 3% of 1, although the orbit sizes differ by a factor of nearly 80. The small differences come from rounding in the listed values, not from a failure of the law.

3 · The inverse-square law

Newton’s law: two point masses pull each other with F = Gm₁m₂/r², along the line joining them. Drag the right-hand mass. Double the distance and the force falls to a quarter; triple it and it falls to a ninth. The two orange arrows are always equal and opposite (Newton’s third law), even when the masses differ.

m₁ m₂

Drag the right mass (or its marker on the curve). Distances in units of d, forces in units of F₀, the force between two unit masses d apart.

Why 1/r²? Picture the pull spreading out over a sphere round the mass: at twice the distance the same “pull” is spread over four times the area. Newton checked it with the Moon, which is about 60 Earth radii away: its acceleration towards the Earth is close to g/60² (try the quiz).

In vector form, the force on m₂ is F = −Gm₁m₂ r̂/r², where r̂ points from m₁ to m₂: the minus sign means “towards m₁”. Gravity is always attractive.

This is the principle of superposition: each pair of masses interacts as if the others were not there, and the net force is the vector sum. It is what lets us add up the pulls of all the bits of a planet in the next section.

4 · Inside and outside a hollow shell

A planet is not a point. To find its pull you must add the pulls of all its parts. Newton proved two neat results for a thin uniform spherical shell: from outside, it pulls exactly as if all its mass were at its centre; from inside, the pulls cancel completely. Drag the test mass P in and out.

pull of a patch of shellnet pull, from adding up the whole shell

The graph is found by adding up the pulls of thin rings round the shell, so it is a real calculation, not a sketch. A solid sphere is a set of nested shells, so it too pulls outside points as if all its mass were at its centre. That is why we can treat the Earth and the Sun as points in sections 1–3.

Unlike charge inside a metal box, a mass inside a shell is not shielded from other bodies outside it. The shell only cancels its own pull. There is no way to screen off gravity.

Weighing the force: Cavendish (1798)

Schematic, not to scale, seen from above.

A light bar with two small lead balls hangs from a thin wire. Two big lead balls are placed near them, on opposite sides. Their pulls form a couple that twists the bar until the wire’s restoring torque balances it: G(Mm/d²)·L = τθ, where τ is the wire’s torque per unit angle, measured separately. Measure θ, and G follows.

G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². Two 1 kg masses 1 m apart pull each other with , the weight of about . Gravity between everyday objects is far too weak to notice; it dominates only when one of the masses is a planet.

5 · g on the surface, from G and the Earth’s mass

Treat the Earth as a sphere of mass ME and radius RE. By the shell result it pulls a mass m on its surface with GMEm/RE², and that pull is m g. So g = GME/RE², and it does not depend on m: everything falls with the same acceleration.

The purple arrow (and so the tilt of g′) is drawn 50× larger than to scale, or it would be invisible.

Run it backwards: g and RE are easy to measure, so once Cavendish had G, the Earth’s mass followed: ME = gRE²/G. That is why his experiment is called “weighing the Earth”.

The Earth also spins. At latitude λ part of the pull is used to carry you round a circle of radius R cos λ, so a spring balance reads slightly less: g′ ≈ g − ω²R cos²λ (spherical-Earth model). The effect is largest at the equator and zero at the poles. Measured sea-level g is about 9.780 m s⁻² at the equator and 9.832 m s⁻² at the poles. Rotation explains about two-thirds of that gap; the rest is because the Earth bulges at the equator, so you are farther from the centre there.

6 · g above and below the surface: one graph

Go up and you get farther from all the mass: g falls as 1/r². Go down a mine and the shell of rock above you pulls in every direction and cancels (section 4); only the ball of Earth below your depth pulls you, and it gets smaller. In a uniform Earth, g falls in a straight line to zero at the centre. Drag the marker.

Inside (depth d): the mass below you is M(r/R)³, so g = GM(r/R)³/r² = g(1 − d/R). Outside (height h): g(h) = GM/(R + h)² = g/(1 + h/R)². For h << R this is about g(1 − 2h/R), the dashed line: g falls twice as fast going up as going down, at first. Either way, g is largest right at the surface.

simplified model The straight line inside assumes the Earth has the same density all the way through. The real Earth is much denser near the centre (an iron core), so just below the surface g does not fall like this; it stays close to its surface value for a long way down. Both models agree that g falls to zero at the centre; exam problems use the uniform one.

7 · Potential energy −GMm/r, and where mgh comes from

Lifting a mass away from the Earth takes work, so its potential energy rises with r. But the pull weakens with height, so each extra metre costs less. Choosing U = 0 at infinity (where the pull has died away), the curve is U(r) = −GMm/r: negative everywhere, rising towards zero. Slide the height and compare the true rise in energy with mgh.

U(r) = −GMm/rinside a uniform Earthmgh line (constant pull)

True rise: ΔU = GMm(1/R − 1/(R + h)) = mgh · R/(R + h). When h << R the factor R/(R + h) is almost 1, so ΔU ≈ mgh: the straight mgh line is the tangent to the curve at the surface. It treats the pull as constant, so it overestimates more and more as h grows. At h = R it is out by a factor of 2.

Only differences of U have meaning; the zero is a choice. With zero at infinity, every bound object has negative U. The gravitational potential V = U/m = −GM/r is the energy per kilogram. For a system of several masses, add −Gmimj/rij over every pair. Inside a uniform sphere (faint curve) the potential is −GM(3R² − r²)/2R³, lowest at the centre, 1.5 times the surface value.
