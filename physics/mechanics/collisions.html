Collisions

When two bodies collide, they push on each other with equal and opposite forces for the same short time. So whatever momentum one gains, the other loses: total momentum is always conserved. Kinetic energy is a different story. Some of it can be lost to squashing, heat and sound, and how much is lost decides what kind of collision it is.

Momentum bars: p₁ and p₂ change during the contact, but by equal and opposite amounts, so the total bar never moves, not even mid-collision. Kinetic energy: the bar is one fixed length, shared between K₁, K₂, energy stored in the squashed bumper, and energy lost. Even an elastic collision dips in K during contact; it gets it all back only at the end.

Restitution e = (speed of separation)/(speed of approach) = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂). e = 1: elastic, no K lost. e = 0: perfectly inelastic, the carts stick together. In between: inelastic. With momentum conservation this gives v₁ = [(m₁ − e m₂)u₁ + (1 + e)m₂u₂]/(m₁ + m₂), and the same with 1 ↔ 2 swapped.

Centre-of-mass view. Riding with the centre of mass (speed vcm = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂)), the total momentum is zero: the carts come in with equal and opposite momenta and leave with the velocities simply reversed and scaled by e. All the kinetic energy that can be lost is the energy in this frame, ½µurel² with µ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂); the fraction lost is 1 − e². The motion of the centre of mass itself (½Mvcm²) can never be lost: momentum conservation protects it.

2 · Elastic hits on a body at rest: what the mass ratio does

Cart 1 (mass m₁, speed u) hits cart 2 at rest, elastically. Slide the mass ratio m₂/m₁ and watch both final velocities. Before moving the slider: what happens when the target is very heavy? Very light?

With u₂ = 0 and e = 1: v₁ = (m₁ − m₂)u/(m₁ + m₂), v₂ = 2m₁u/(m₁ + m₂). The target takes the fraction 4m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)² of the kinetic energy, largest (all of it) when the masses are equal. That is why a nuclear reactor slows its neutrons with light nuclei (heavy water, graphite) rather than heavy ones. model nuclei taken as 2 and 12 neutron masses; real collisions there are rarely head-on, so the loss per hit is smaller.

3 · Glancing collision of equal balls: always 90°

A smooth ball hits an identical ball at rest, slightly off-centre. Move the aim. The push between them acts along the line joining their centres, so the target always leaves along that line. Where does the cue ball go?

Why 90°. Momentum: mv1i = mv1f + mv2f, so the three velocities form a triangle. Kinetic energy (elastic): v1i² = v1f² + v2f². That is Pythagoras, so the angle between v1f and v2f is 90°. The corner always lies on the semicircle drawn on v1i.

In two dimensions there are four unknowns after the collision (two speeds, two angles) but only three equations (momentum in x and y, plus energy if elastic). One more fact is needed: here, the aim, which fixes the line of centres. Unequal masses, or a collision that is not elastic, break the 90° rule. model smooth, non-spinning balls; no friction with the table.
