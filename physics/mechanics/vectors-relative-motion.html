Vectors and Relative Motion

On a line, a sign (+ or −) is enough to say which way. In a plane it is not: you need an arrow. This page builds the arrow rules by hand (adding, subtracting, scaling, splitting into components) and then uses them for what they are for: velocity in a plane, and how the same rain or the same river looks different to people who are moving.

1 · Scalars, vectors, and why displacement ignores the path

A scalar is one number with a unit. A vector has a size and a direction, and two of them combine by the triangle rule (section 2). Pick a route from P to Q: the distance walked changes, the displacement arrow does not.

route walkeddisplacement (position vector change)

Displacement is the straight arrow from start to finish, so its size can never exceed the path length. They are equal only if you never turn. A round trip has zero displacement: the null vector, which has no direction at all.

2 · Adding, subtracting, scaling: drag the arrow heads

Drag the round handles at the tips of A and B. A vector is only a length and a direction, so sliding it parallel to itself leaves it the same vector. That is what lets us put B’s tail on A’s head.

3 · Components: turn the arrow

Any arrow in the plane is “so much along î plus so much along ĵ”, where î and ĵ are arrows of length 1 along x and y. Turn A all the way round and watch the components shrink, vanish and change sign.

In three dimensions the same idea adds k̂ along z: A = Axî + Ayĵ + Azk̂, with |A| = √(Ax² + Ay² + Az²). A unit vector along A is n̂ = A/|A|: same direction, length 1, no unit.

4 · Position, displacement and velocity on a curved path

A particle moves so that x = 3t and y = 2t² (metres, t in seconds). Its position vector r runs from the origin to the particle. Shrink the time gap Δt and watch the average velocity Δr/Δt swing round onto the tangent.

position r, displacement ΔrvelocityΔv (sets the acceleration)

In components: v = (dx/dt) î + (dy/dt) ĵ = 3 î + 4t ĵ m s⁻¹, and a = dv/dt = 4 ĵ m s⁻². x and y behave as two separate straight-line motions happening at once: x at steady speed, y with steady acceleration. Unlike motion on a line, v and a here point in different directions; in a plane the angle between them can be anything from 0° to 180°.

5 · Rain and an umbrella: two observers, one rain

Velocity always means velocity relative to someone. The rain’s velocity relative to you is vrain − vyou: you subtract your own motion. 6 · Crossing a river: shortest time or shortest path?

The boat’s engine gives it speed v relative to the water; the water itself moves downstream at u. Relative to the ground the boat moves at vboat + u. Choose where to point the boat, then cross. Left: seen from the bank. Right: seen by someone drifting on a raft.

Seen from the bank

Seen from a raft drifting with the water

Only the across-component v cos α carries the boat over, and the current cannot help or hurt it, so the time is d/(v cos α): shortest when α = 0, pointing straight across, whatever u is. The drift is (u − v sin α) × time. To land straight opposite, aim upstream so that v sin α = u. That needs v > u; if the river is faster than the boat, the least drift comes from sin α = v/u instead.

Models used: point particles; velocities constant (steady rain, steady wind, a river flowing at the same speed everywhere across its width, which real rivers do not: they are slower near the banks); speeds much less than light, so velocities simply subtract.
