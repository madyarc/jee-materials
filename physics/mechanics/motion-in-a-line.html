Motion in a Straight Line

Before asking why things move (that is Newton’s laws), we need a precise way to say how they move. Along a straight line that takes one number, the position x, and how it changes with time. Velocity is how fast x changes; acceleration is how fast velocity changes. Three graphs, x–t, v–t and a–t, carry the whole story. Each one is the slope of the one above it, and the area under the one below it. The object is treated as a point, which is fine whenever its size is small next to the distance it moves.

Distance vs displacement. Displacement is the signed area under v–t: area below the axis counts negative, because the car is then moving backwards. Distance (path length) adds every bit of area as positive. They are equal only if the car never turns back; otherwise distance is larger. The same goes for average speed (distance ÷ time) and the size of the average velocity (displacement ÷ time).

Sharp corners on these graphs are a simplified model: here a jumps suddenly at each blue dot. In a real car, v and a change smoothly, so the corners are rounded.

2 · Average velocity → instantaneous velocity

The car’s position is x = 0.08 t³ (x in m, t in s). The average velocity over an interval is the slope of the chord P₁P₂. Shrink the interval Δt round the instant t and watch the chord swing into the tangent at P.

Speed is the size of velocity, so +3.8 m s⁻¹ and −3.8 m s⁻¹ both mean 3.8 m s⁻¹. At a single instant the two can never disagree: over a vanishing interval there is no time to turn back, so path length and the size of the displacement become the same.

The same limit on a v–t graph gives the acceleration: chord slope Δv/Δt is the average acceleration, tangent slope dv/dt is the acceleration at that instant.

3 · The equations of motion, read off one picture

With constant acceleration the v–t graph is a straight line. Everything else is geometry: its slope, its end height and the area under it. Change u, a and t and read each equation off the shape.

Slope of the line is a, so after time t its height is v = u + at.

Area = rectangle u·t (blue) + triangle ½·t·(at) (pink), so s = ut + ½at².

The dashed line at the mean height ½(u + v) makes a rectangle of the same area, so s = ½(u + v)t: average velocity is the mean of the two ends. This holds only because the line is straight (constant a).

Use t = (v − u)/a in that last one to remove t: s = (v + u)(v − u)/2a, so v² = u² + 2as.

All the quantities are signed: choose a positive direction first. These four only hold for constant a. The definitions v = dx/dt and a = dv/dt always hold, and so does a = v·dv/dx, which handles an acceleration that depends on position.

4 · Thrown up, falling down

A ball thrown straight up slows, stops for an instant, and falls back. Throughout, its acceleration is the same: g, downward. At the top v = 0, but a is not zero; otherwise the ball would hang there for ever. Set the throw and play it; the strobe dots are its positions at equal time steps.

Braking from u to rest: 0 = u² − 2b·s, so s = u²/2b. Double the speed and the braking distance becomes four times as long, while the reaction distance only doubles.

Steady braking (constant deceleration) is a simplified model; it is why the measured points sit near, not exactly on, one v² curve.

6 · Relative velocity: two cars

Car A and car B drive along the same straight road. Watch from the roadside, then ride in car A. From A, the road slides backwards at −vA and B moves at vB − vA. For motion in a plane, see Vectors and relative motion.

On the x–t graph, each car’s velocity is the slope of its line. The gap xB − xA changes at the difference of the slopes, vBA = vB − vA. Parallel lines: same velocity, the gap never changes. Lines crossing: one car passes the other at that instant.

Models used: point objects moving along one straight line; piecewise-constant acceleration in section 1 (real graphs have rounded corners); constant acceleration in sections 3–5; free fall with no air resistance and constant g near the ground (g = 9.8 m s⁻² unless a problem says 10); steady braking at constant deceleration; the ruler drop assumes it falls freely from rest.
