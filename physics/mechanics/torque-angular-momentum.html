Torque and Angular Momentum

Turning has its own versions of force, momentum and Newton’s second law. Every one is built the same way: take the straight-line quantity, and weight it by how far from the axis it acts. That “how far” needs a new way to multiply vectors, the cross product. Build it, then use it to open doors, balance beams and spin up a swivel chair.

1 · Rotation about a fixed axis

A disc turns about a fixed axis through its centre (you are looking straight down the axis). Every point moves in a circle round the axis. Spin it with the slider: which points move fastest?

disc radius 0.30 m

One angle θ describes the whole body, so one ω = dθ/dt (and one angular acceleration α = dω/dt) belongs to every point. Their speeds differ: v = ωr, where r is the distance from the axis. As a vector, ω points along the axis by the right-hand rule: curl your right-hand fingers the way it turns, and your thumb gives ω. Then v = ω × r.

A rigid body is the model here: distances between its particles never change. Real bodies bend a little. An axis need not be fixed in every case: a spinning top keeps only one point (its tip) fixed, and its axis sweeps round a cone (precession). This page sticks to a fixed axis.

2 · The cross product is an area with a direction

Drag the tips of a and b. The shaded parallelogram’s area is |a × b| = ab sin θ. The result points straight out of the screen (⊙) or into it (⊗).

Rules that follow from the picture: a × a = 0 (no area); b × a = −a × b (the curl reverses); a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c. In components, a × b = (aybz − azby, azbx − axbz, axby − aybx), the determinant with î, ĵ, k̂ in the top row. The triangle with sides a and b has half the area, ½|a × b|; and a·(b × c) is the volume of the box built on a, b, c. Reverse both vectors, as a reflection through the origin does (a → −a, b → −b), and a × b does not change.

3 · Torque: where and how you push

A door seen from above, hinged at H. Drag the point where you push along the door, and drag the tip of the force arrow to change its size and direction. Before you try: what does a push straight along the door do?

r, from the hingeforce Flever arm r⊥ (from H to the line of F)

τ = r × F. Its size can be read two ways, both on the picture: τ = r⊥F (the whole force times the lever arm) or τ = rF⊥ (the whole distance times the part of F across the door). The unit is N m. That is the same unit as the joule, but torque is a vector and not energy: nothing has moved yet. Reverse F and τ reverses.

4 · Angular momentum of a moving particle

A particle (1 kg) moves at constant velocity along a straight line. Does it have angular momentum about a point O that is not on the line? Is it changing? Drag O, play, then try the other cases.

l = r × p, of size l = r⊥p: momentum times the perpendicular distance from O to its line. Differentiate: dl/dt = v × mv + r × F. The first term is zero (parallel vectors), so dl/dt = r × F = τ, the rotational form of F = dp/dt. For many particles add them up: L = Σli. Internal forces act in equal, opposite pairs along the line joining the two particles, so their torques cancel, leaving dL/dt = τext.

5 · Conservation: the swivel chair

Someone spins on a frictionless swivel chair holding a dumbbell in each hand. Nothing outside twists the chair, so τext = 0 and L = Iω stays fixed. Predict: arms pulled in, does the spin speed up or slow down? Does the kinetic energy change?

illustrative numbers: I₀ = 1.2 kg m² (person + chair), two 2 kg dumbbells, ω = 2.0 rad/s with arms at 0.80 m

I = I₀ + 2mr² (why mass far out counts as r²: Moment of Inertia). For fixed-axis rotation, Lz = Iω, and dLz/dt = τz. Divers, skaters, dancers in a pirouette all use this: tuck in to spin fast, open out to slow down. Strictly, L lies along the axis only for a body symmetric about it; for a lopsided one, L and ω point in different directions.

When a torque does act, L changes in its direction. If the torque is at right angles to L (a spinning bicycle wheel held by one end of its axle), L turns round instead of growing: the axle swings sideways. That is precession, the same thing a top does.

6 · Equilibrium: no net force AND no net torque

A rigid body stays at rest only if both add to zero: ΣF = 0 (it does not start moving off) and Στ = 0 (it does not start turning). Either can hold without the other.

7 · Rotational kinematics: the same graphs, new letters

With constant angular acceleration α, angular velocity grows in a straight line, exactly like v with constant a. The area under the ω–t graph is the angle turned, just as the area under v–t is the distance.

ω = ω₀ + αt is the straight line. θ = ω₀t + ½αt² is the shaded area (a rectangle plus a triangle). Eliminate t and you get ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ. These are v = u + at, s = ut + ½at² and v² = u² + 2as with θ for s, ω for v, α for a. To use them, angles must be in radians: 1 rev = 2π rad, so ω in rad/s = 2π × (rpm)/60.

8 · Translation and rotation, side by side

Left: a 20 kg block pulled by 25 N on a frictionless floor. Right: a 20 kg flywheel of radius 0.20 m (a disc, I = ½MR² = 0.4 kg m²), with a cord round its rim pulled with the same 25 N. Run them together and read across each row.

Why τ = Iα and not something else: the work done by a torque turning a body through dθ is τ dθ (the force times the arc r dθ its point moves). So power is τω. With no friction all of it becomes kinetic energy, d(½Iω²)/dt = Iωα. Set τω = Iωα and τ = Iα drops out. (The kinetic energy of rotation itself: each bit moves at v = ωr, so K = Σ½mr²ω² = ½(Σmr²)ω² = ½Iω².) For a fixed axis, only the part of a torque along the axis turns the body; the bearings supply whatever is needed to cancel the rest, which would only try to tilt the axle.
