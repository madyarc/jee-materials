Projectiles and Circular Motion

Two curved paths, each built from straight-line ideas. A thrown ball is a steady sideways drift and a free fall happening at the same time. A ball whirled on a string keeps its speed, yet it is accelerating all the time, because the direction of its velocity keeps turning toward the centre. For the vector tools used here, see Vectors and Relative Motion.

1 · Launch a projectile no air resistance

Horizontal: no force, so vx = u cos θ stays fixed and x = (u cos θ) t. Vertical: free fall, vy = u sin θ − gt and y = (u sin θ) t − ½gt². Eliminate t and the path is

y = (a number) × x − (a number) × x²: a parabola. At the top vy = 0, after tm = u sin θ/g; from level ground the flight lasts T = 2tm, the top is H = (u sin θ)²/2g high, and the range is R = u² sin 2θ/g. Since sin 2θ = sin(180° − 2θ), the angles θ and 90° − θ give the same range, and the largest, u²/g, comes at 45°. Launch from a height and the ball has extra time to travel, so a flatter angle (below 45°) goes furthest.

2 · Throwing up a slope no air resistance

Now the ground is an incline at angle α, and the throw is measured from the slope: β above it. The trick is to tilt the axes so x′ runs along the slope and y′ straight out of it. Gravity then has two parts, and each direction becomes a simple straight-line motion again.

path; dots at equal time stepsdots’ shadows on the slopeg and its two parts

Distance up the slope for every β

Perpendicular to the slope (y′): starts at u sin β, pulled back by g cos α. It returns to the slope after T = 2u sin β/(g cos α), longer than on level ground, because g cos α is weaker than g. Along the slope (x′): starts at u cos β, slowed by g sin α, so the shadows bunch up. Distance up the slope: R = u²[sin(2β + α) − sin α]/(g cos²α). It is greatest when 2β + α = 90°, that is β = 45° − α/2: the throw bisects the angle between the slope and the vertical, and then Rmax = u²/[g(1 + sin α)]. Set α = 0 and all of this turns back into level ground.

3 · Two projectiles at once: watch from one of them no air resistance

Throw A and B at the same instant. From the ground both paths curve. Now ride along with A: both balls fall with the same g, so the falling cancels, and B moves in a straight line at constant velocity, vB − vA. Whether they hit is just whether that straight line passes through A.

4 · Uniform circular motion: constant speed, turning velocity

The ball goes round at constant speed v. Its velocity is always along the tangent, so its direction keeps changing, and a change in velocity is an acceleration. Open the angle Δθ between two moments and see where the change Δv points.

position r, r′velocity v, v′Δv and acceleration
Shown 3× slower than real time.

Why v²/r: the radius turns through Δθ and so does the velocity, which stays at right angles to it. The triangle of r, r′, Δr and the triangle of v, v′, Δv have the same shape, so Δv/v = Δr/r. For small Δθ the chord Δr is the arc, v Δt. So |Δv|/Δt → v²/r. With v = ωr this is also ω²r = 4π²ν²r.

The size of this acceleration is constant but its direction keeps turning, so it is not a constant vector, and v = u + at and the other constant-acceleration equations do not apply to circular motion.

Models used: point particle; no air resistance (a real ball falls short of these paths, more so at high speed); g = 9.8 m s⁻² everywhere, uniform and vertical, over ground taken as flat (or, in section 2, a flat slope). In section 3 the balls count as colliding if their centres come within 0.5 m.
