Friction and Circular Motion

Friction is the part of the contact force that lies along the surface. It has no fixed size: static friction is exactly as large as it needs to be, up to a limit. That one idea explains why a pushed block stays put, why a parked car stays on a slope, and why a car can turn a corner at all. For a turn, something has to pull the car toward the centre, and on a flat road friction is the only candidate.

1 · Static friction adjusts itself, up to a limit

Push a block resting on the floor. Slide the push up slowly and watch the friction arrow. Predict first: what happens at the moment the push passes the limit?

While the block is still, friction fs exactly cancels your push, so it is equal to F, however small F is. It can grow only up to a limit, fs ≤ µsN. Push past that and the block breaks free; friction then drops to the kinetic value fk = µkN (µk < µs), and the block accelerates at (F − fk)/m.

Friction opposes relative motion (or, while still, the motion that would happen without it). The laws fs ≤ µsN and fk = µkN are empirical, approximate: not fundamental laws like gravity. To a good approximation both are independent of the contact area, and fk barely depends on speed. At the atomic level, contact forces are electrical attractions and repulsions.

Once sliding, you can ease off to F = µkN and it keeps sliding at constant speed; this graph shows a block that starts from rest each time.

2 · A block on a rough slope; the angle of repose

Tilt the slope. Gravity's pull along the slope, mg sin θ, grows; the most that static friction can give, µsmg cos θ, shrinks. Where they cross, the block starts to slide. Add a push along the slope and friction may even flip direction.

friction needed to hold itmost static friction can give, µsNslides

With no push the block slips when mg sin θ = µsmg cos θ, i.e. at the angle of repose θr = tan⁻¹ µs. The mass cancels: a heavy and a light block of the same material slip together. Tilting a plank until a block slides is a simple way to measure µs.

Model: block 2 kg (it does not change the angle), µk = 0.8 µs chosen for illustration only, g = 9.8 m s⁻². Push: + up the slope, − down it.

3 · Rolling friction, and friction as friend and foe

An ideal hard wheel rolling on a hard floor touches it at one point, which is momentarily at rest, so nothing slides and no friction is needed to keep it rolling. Real wheels and floors squash a little.

Schematic, deformation hugely exaggerated.

In real rolling the contact is a small patch, not a point. The ground pushes harder on the front of the patch than on the back, so the total contact force leans slightly backward. Its backward part is rolling friction. For the same weight it is far smaller than sliding friction, in good cases by two or three orders of magnitude (100 to 1000 times). That is why the wheel, and ball bearings, matter so much.

Foe: friction wastes energy as heat in machines; lubricants, ball bearings and air cushions reduce it. Friend: brakes rely on kinetic friction; walking and driving need static friction (the road pushes the tyre forward); a car cannot move on a perfectly slippery road. More on rolling: Rolling Motion.

4 · "Centripetal force" is a job, not a new force

Moving in a circle at steady speed, a body's velocity keeps turning, so it accelerates toward the centre at v²/R. By the second law something must pull it inward with fc = mv²/R. That something is always a real force you can name. Remove it and the body flies off along the tangent.

velocity (along the tangent)the real inward force

Don't add "centripetal force" as an extra arrow in a free-body diagram. Draw the real forces; their component toward the centre is mv²/R. The outward push you feel on a merry-go-round is not a force on you: it is your body's tendency to go straight on (inertia), while the seat or wall pulls you inward.

5 · A car turning on a level road

On a flat road the only horizontal force on the car is friction from the road on the tyres, so friction must supply all of mv²/R. The tyres are not sliding sideways, so it is static friction, and it has a ceiling.

Needed: mv²/R. Available: at most µsN = µsmg. So the turn is safe while v² ≤ µsRg, giving vmax = √(µsRg). The mass cancels, so a loaded truck and a small car share the same limit (bars are per kilogram). Too fast or too tight, and the car slides outward onto a wider curve.

6 · Banked roads: let the normal force help

Tilt the road toward the centre of the turn. Now the normal force leans inward too, and its horizontal part supplies some or all of mv²/R. At one speed, the optimum vopt, no friction is needed at all. Slower than that, friction must act up the slope; faster, down the slope.

Vertical: N cos θ = mg + f sin θ. Toward the centre: N sin θ + f cos θ = mv²/R (f taken down the slope). With f = 0: vopt = √(Rg tan θ). With f at its limit µsN, down the slope: vmax = √(Rg(µs + tan θ)/(1 − µs tan θ)), larger than on a flat road. Up the slope: vmin = √(Rg(tan θ − µs)/(1 + µs tan θ)).

The graph plots the friction the car needs as a fraction of N. Inside the band ±µs, static friction can supply it. If tan θ ≤ µs there is no lower limit: the car can even be parked on the bank. Driving near vopt means little sideways force on the tyres, so less wear.
