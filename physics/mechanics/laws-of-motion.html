Newton's Laws

A force is not what keeps things moving. A force is what changes motion. Galileo saw this by imagining friction away. Newton then made it exact: force is the rate of change of momentum, and forces always come in pairs between two bodies. This page builds each law as a picture, then puts them to work in free-body diagrams.

1 · Aristotle's mistake and Galileo's double incline

Aristotle taught that a moving body needs a force to keep it moving, and everyday life seems to agree: a toy car stops when you let go of the string. Galileo's test: let a ball roll down one ramp and up another. Make the second ramp shallower and shallower. How far does it go when the ramp is flat?

friction:

Galileo's first observations: going down a slope the ball speeds up, going up it slows down, so on a level surface it should do neither. Without friction the ball always climbs back to its starting height, however shallow the ramp; a shallower ramp only means a longer path. At 0° there is no height to regain, so it never stops. That is the law of inertia: with no net force, a body keeps its velocity. Rest and steady straight-line motion are the same state, both with zero acceleration. Friction, not a missing push, is what stops real bodies.

Simplified model: the ball is treated as a point; losses are a small sliding-friction coefficient µ standing in for rolling friction and air drag. Start height 1 m, first ramp 40°, g = 9.8 m s⁻².

Newton's first law: every body stays at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless an external force compels it to change that state. In short: zero net external force ⇔ zero acceleration.

2 · Momentum, impulse and the second law

Stopping a fast cricket ball hurts; stopping it slowly does not. Both remove the same momentum p = mv. What changes is how quickly. Force is the rate of change of momentum.

The two shaded areas are equal: impulse = area under the F–t graph = FavgΔt = Δp. The ball must lose the same momentum either way, so drawing the hands back (a longer Δt) spreads the same area out low and wide. Lower force, less pain.

Newton's second law: F = dp/dt. For a fixed mass, dp/dt = m dv/dt, so F = ma. The newton is defined by it: 1 N gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻². It is a vector law, one equation for each component.

Model: 0.15 kg ball; the force pulse is drawn as a smooth hump (half a sine wave, a simplified shape); "give" assumes steady slowing.

Same force, same time, different masses

Both carts feel 2 N for the same time. The light one ends up faster, the heavy one slower, yet the momentum bars always match: F Δt = Δp, whatever the mass.

That is why momentum, not velocity, is the natural quantity in the second law. A heavier body at the same speed needs more force to stop in the same time; so does a faster body of the same mass.

3 · Third law: every force has a partner on the other body

A book rests on a table on the Earth (pulled apart so you can see each body). Pick a body to see the forces on it. Then tap one of its forces: its third-law partner lights up, and it is always on a different body.

Schematic, not to scale. Arrow lengths: book's weight = 1 unit, table's weight = 3 units; the Earth's own weight is not drawn.

Forces on the book

Fon A by B = −Fon B by A. Same size, opposite direction, same instant (neither one "causes" the other), and on different bodies. So the two forces of a pair can never cancel each other.

The book's weight and the table's push are equal only because the book is at rest (first law); they are not a pair. In an accelerating lift they differ (section 6). Choose "book + table" and the pair between them becomes internal: it cancels inside the system.

4 · Conservation of momentum: recoil and break-up

A gun fires a shell; a nucleus splits; two skaters push apart. During the push, the forces on the two pieces are a third-law pair: equal, opposite and acting for the same time. So their momentum changes are equal and opposite, and the total stays what it was: zero.

pA + pB = 0 before, so pA + pB = 0 after: mAvA = −mBvB. The heavier piece moves slower, in exact inverse ratio of the masses. The cross (centre of mass) never moves.

The law holds for any isolated system (no net external force), in every collision, elastic or not. Collisions get their own page: Collisions.

5 · Equilibrium: forces that close into a triangle

A particle is in equilibrium when the net force on it is zero. Draw its forces head to tail: if they end where they started, the net force is zero. Three forces make a closed triangle, more forces a closed polygon.

Drag the arrow tips.

In components: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 (and ΣFz = 0). For an extended body, zero net force is not enough: the turning effects must also cancel (see Torque and Angular Momentum).

6 · Free-body diagram builder

The method for every mechanics problem: pick one body (or a group of bodies) as your system, then draw only the forces on it by everything else. Choose a set-up, tap a body, predict, then see the diagram to scale with the acceleration and tensions worked out.

Rules used: light inextensible strings (same tension all along, same acceleration at both ends), light frictionless pulleys, rigid blocks treated as particles. A light spring would pull with F = −kx (x = stretch); a string is a very stiff spring, so its stretch is ignored. Air drag, buoyancy and viscosity are contact forces too, left out here.

Models used: point particles and rigid blocks; light inextensible strings and frictionless light pulleys; no air resistance; in section 1, losses modelled as a constant friction coefficient; friction in the pulley set-up uses one coefficient for both static and sliding friction (a simplification). g = 9.8 m s⁻² unless a question states 10.
