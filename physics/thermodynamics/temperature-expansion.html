Temperature and Thermal Expansion

How hot something is gets a number from a thermometer, and every thermometer relies on something that changes with temperature. This page builds the temperature scales, finds absolute zero from a gas thermometer, then shows why almost everything grows when heated, why water is the odd one out, and what happens when expansion is blocked.

1 · Temperature is not heat

A small cup of hot tea touches a big bucket of warm water. The bucket holds far more energy. Before you press the button: which way does heat flow, and when does it stop?

Temperature says how hot a body is. Heat is energy that moves from one body to another because their temperatures differ. It always flows from the higher temperature to the lower one, however big or small the bodies are, and stops when the temperatures are equal (thermal equilibrium).

So the small hot cup warms the big bucket, even though the bucket has much more energy in it. The bigger body’s temperature changes less. Heat is measured in joules (J); temperature in kelvin (K) or degrees Celsius (°C).

The final temperature here assumes both are water with no losses. How to calculate it: Calorimetry and change of state. Why a thermometer can be trusted at all: the zeroth law in Laws of thermodynamics.

2 · Thermometers and temperature scales

A thermometer uses a property that changes steadily with temperature, such as the volume of mercury or alcohol in a glass tube. To put numbers on it you need two fixed points that always happen at the same temperature: the ice point (pure water freezing) and the steam point (pure water boiling), both at standard atmospheric pressure. Drag the marker up and down: one temperature, four names for it.

Make your own scale JEE

Calibrating: put the bulb in melting ice and mark the liquid level; put it in steam and mark again. Divide the gap into 100 equal steps for Celsius or 180 for Fahrenheit (which calls the two marks 32 and 212). A liquid thermometer only works while its liquid stays a liquid, so no single liquid covers every temperature.

Fahrenheit against Celsius: a straight line

The same 100 °C interval is 180 °F, so the slope is 180/100 = 9/5, and the line passes through (0, 32): (tF − 32)/180 = tC/100, i.e. tF = (9/5)tC + 32.

3 · A gas thermometer finds absolute zero

Liquid thermometers made with different liquids disagree slightly between the fixed points, because each liquid expands in its own way. Gases at low density all behave alike. Keep a gas at constant volume and read the temperature from its pressure. Below are three samples (different gases or different amounts, all at low density). Change the bath temperature, then extend the lines.

solid: range a real gas thermometer can measure; dashed: extrapolation

The Kelvin (absolute) scale starts at absolute zero and uses the Celsius-sized degree: T = tC + 273.15. On this scale every line above passes through the origin, so at constant volume P ∝ T. That is the ideal-gas equation PV = µRT (µ moles, R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) with V held fixed, and it is why a gas thermometer measures absolute temperature directly. Real gases stop following the line at low temperature (they liquefy), which is why the dashed part is an extrapolation. More on the gas law: Kinetic theory.

One fixed point is enough. Once zero is fixed at absolute zero, a single reference sets the size of the degree. The Kelvin scale uses the triple point of water, the one temperature and pressure (611.657 Pa, about 0.006 atm) at which ice, liquid water and water vapour coexist, and assigns it 273.16 K (0.01 °C). It is easier to reproduce than the ice point and steam point, which shift with air pressure. With this choice, ice melts at very nearly 0 °C and water boils at very nearly 100 °C at 1 atm, which is why T = tC + 273.15 uses 273.15, not 273.16.

4 · Why a solid grows when heated JEE

The atoms of a solid sit in a row, each vibrating about its place. The graph shows the potential energy of two neighbours against their separation r. Heating gives them more vibration energy, which raises the horizontal energy line. Watch the middle of the swing (the dot).

The curve is steep on the left (atoms pushed together repel strongly) and gentle on the right (pulling them apart is easier). At higher energy the atoms swing further, and because the right wall is gentler, the swing extends more to the right than to the left. The average separation grows, and a whole crystal of such pairs gets bigger.

6 · Who expands most: solids, liquids, gases

The volume expansion coefficient αV = (ΔV/V)/ΔT for common substances. Each grid line is ×10. Before you look: where would a gas sit compared with water?

solidsliquidsgaslog scale: each grid line is ×10

Solids < liquids < gases. Metals expand more than glass; invar, an iron–nickel alloy, barely expands at all, which is why it is used in measuring tapes and clock pendulums. Pyrex glass expands much less than ordinary glass, so it survives sudden heating. Liquids expand more than solids (alcohol more than mercury). A gas expands most of all.

Ideal gas at constant pressure: PV = µRT gives PΔV = µRΔT, so ΔV/V = ΔT/T and αV = 1/T. At 0 °C that is ; at 27 °C it is . It falls as the gas gets hotter.

αV is not quite a constant

schematic, shape only For copper, αV falls towards zero as T approaches 0 K and becomes nearly constant at high temperature. Over 0–100 °C it changes little, which is why a single average value works for everyday problems.

7 · Water, the exception: densest at 4 °C

Cool almost any liquid and it shrinks. Water shrinks too, down to about 4 °C. Below that it expands as it cools further, all the way to freezing. Drag along the curve.

If water behaved like other liquids, the coldest water would sink, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, and much of the plant and animal life in them would die. Ice also conducts heat poorly, so the ice layer slows further freezing: see the pond in Heat transfer.

8 · Blocked expansion: thermal stress

A steel rail 5 m long with a 40 cm² cross-section is heated. Free, it simply gets longer. Clamp it between rigid walls and it cannot: the walls must push it back to its old length, and that push is the thermal stress.

Fitting an iron ring on a cart wheel

Where it matters. Railway lines are laid with small gaps between rails so they can expand on hot days without buckling. Bridges have expansion joints. A tight metal lid on a glass jar loosens in hot water because the metal expands more than the glass.
