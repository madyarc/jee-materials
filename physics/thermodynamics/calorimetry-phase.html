Heat, Calorimetry and Change of State

Put the same heat into two different things and their temperatures change by very different amounts. Keep heating ice and the temperature stops rising twice, while the heat goes into breaking the ice apart and then into boiling the water. Squeeze ice and it can melt. This page builds each of these as a picture you can change. (How temperature is measured and how things expand when heated are on Temperature and Thermal Expansion.)

1 · Same heat, very different warming

Four blocks of equal mass sit on identical heaters and each receives the same amount of heat. Before you move the slider: which thermometer will rise the most, and which the least?

For the same heat ΔQ, the rise is ΔT = ΔQ/(m s). A material with a large specific heat capacity s soaks up a lot of heat per degree, so its thermometer barely moves. Water is the extreme case: it needs about times as much heat as aluminium and about times as much as copper for the same rise.

Why water’s high s matters. A car radiator uses water because each kilogram carries away a lot of heat for a small rise in temperature; a hot-water bag stays warm for the same reason. The sea warms and cools far more slowly than the land, which drives the sea breeze by day and the land breeze by night (see Heat Transfer, section 5). Deserts, with little water, heat up fast in the day and cool fast at night.

Values: specific heats at room temperature and 1 atm. Real specific heats change a little with temperature; here they are taken as constant.

Three ways to count “how much heat per degree”

heat capacity
S = ΔQ/ΔT  (J K⁻¹)
specific heat
s = ΔQ/(m ΔT)  (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
molar heat
C = ΔQ/(μ ΔT)  (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)

μ is the number of moles. C = s × (molar mass in kg mol⁻¹).

Gases need two molar heats: Cp and Cv

Cv: all the heat raises internal energyextra at constant pressure: work done expanding

A gas can be heated in a sealed rigid box (constant volume) or under a free piston (constant pressure). At constant pressure the gas also expands and pushes the piston, so part of the heat leaves as work and more heat is needed per degree. For every gas in the table, Cp − Cv comes out close to R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹: the extra work per mole per kelvin of an ideal gas.

Why the gap is exactly R: First Law and PV Diagrams, section 5. Why helium’s Cv is about (3/2)R while N₂’s is about (5/2)R: Degrees of Freedom.

2 · Calorimetry: heat lost = heat gained

Drop a hot metal block into cold water in a calorimeter. Heat flows from block to water until they reach the same temperature. If no heat escapes (an isolated system), every joule the block loses, the water and the vessel gain. Drag the cursor on the graph to guess the final temperature; the two dots must meet.

The answer is a weighted average of the two starting temperatures, weighted by heat capacity: it lands nearer whichever side can store more heat per degree. That is the steeper line on the graph.

The calorimeter. A copper or aluminium vessel with a stirrer of the same metal, inside a wooden jacket packed with an insulator such as glass wool. The jacket is a heat shield: it keeps the system close to isolated. A thermometer goes in through the lid. Because the vessel warms up with the water, it takes some of the heat too; the dashed line on the graph is what the water alone would take.

Water equivalent JEE The vessel (mass mc, specific heat sc) absorbs as much heat per degree as a mass W = mcsc/sw of water would. So in any calculation you can replace the vessel by W of extra water. A copper vessel is a good choice: its small s makes W small. In grams, W is just mcsc with s in cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹, since water’s is 1.

3 · Heating ice until it becomes steam

1 kg of ice at −20 °C is heated steadily at 1 atm until it is steam at 120 °C. Drag along the graph to add heat. The horizontal axis is drawn to scale: notice how much of it is spent at constant temperature.

Drag on the graph, or use the arrow keys after tapping it.

Flat parts: a change of state. While ice melts, ice and water coexist at 0 °C and the temperature does not move, however much heat goes in; the same while water boils at 100 °C. The heat goes into pulling the molecules apart, not into making them move faster. The heat per kilogram is the latent heat L, and Q = mL. For water, Lf = 3.33 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ (fusion) and Lv = 22.6 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ (vaporisation): boiling away water takes times the heat that melting the ice did.

Sloped parts: one phase warming. Here Q = m s ΔT, so the slope ΔT/ΔQ = 1/(m s). Ice and steam have about half the specific heat of liquid water, so their parts of the line are about twice as steep. On the whole curve they are the short steep bits at the two ends; zoom in to see them.

Cooling runs the same curve backwards: steam gives out Lv per kilogram as it condenses, and water gives out Lf as it freezes. Latent heats are quoted at 1 atm; they change with pressure (section 7).

For every substance, boiling takes far more energy than melting: in a liquid the molecules are still touching, and only boiling pulls them fully apart against their attraction.

4 · Why a steam burn is worse than a boiling-water burn

Both are at 100 °C. Suppose 10 g of each lands on your skin and ends up at body temperature, 37 °C. Predict first: how many times more heat does the steam deliver?

Water only cools from 100 °C to 37 °C. Steam first has to condense on the skin, releasing its latent heat, and then the condensed water cools as well.

Rough model: all the heat is taken to go into the skin.

5 · Ice in warm water: does it all melt? JEE

The common trap: writing “heat lost = heat gained” with an unknown final temperature and getting an answer below 0 °C (or a negative mass). First compare the heat the water can give with the heat the ice needs.

Recipe. (1) Heat the water gives cooling to 0 °C: mwswTw. (2) Heat the ice needs to reach 0 °C, then to melt: misi|Ti| and miLf. If (1) is less than the total of (2), the mixture ends at 0 °C with some ice left, and the leftover follows from the energy that is short. Only if (1) is bigger do you solve for a final temperature above 0 °C.

6 · Phase diagrams: where pressure enters

Melting and boiling points depend on pressure. A pressure–temperature map shows which state is stable at every (T, P). Three curves divide it: on each curve, two states coexist. Drag the point; then use the guide lines to heat at fixed pressure or squeeze at fixed temperature.

schematic, not to scale

Why water’s fusion curve leans backwards. Ice is less dense than water (it floats). Squeezing favours the denser state, which for water is the liquid, so more pressure means a lower melting point: about 0.0073 °C less for each extra atmosphere. The tilt is tiny; the diagram exaggerates it. For most substances, CO₂ included, the solid is the denser state, so their fusion curve leans forwards and pressure raises the melting point.

Sublimation. Below the triple-point pressure there is no liquid at all: a solid heated there turns straight into vapour. CO₂’s triple point is at 5.11 atm, above ordinary air pressure, so solid CO₂ (dry ice) sublimes at −78.5 °C and never melts in the open. Iodine is also used to show sublimation: it gives off violet vapour well below its melting point. But its triple point (12.1 kPa) is below 1 atm, so heated in a closed tube iodine does melt, at 114 °C.

The normal melting and boiling points are those at 1 atm (101.325 kPa). The triple point is the one (T, P) where solid, liquid and vapour all coexist. Above the critical point the liquid and vapour become indistinguishable, and no amount of squeezing will condense the gas into a separate liquid.

7 · Boiling point and pressure: hills and pressure cookers

Water boils when its vapour pressure reaches the pressure pushing down on it. Lower the outside pressure and it boils cooler; raise it and it must get hotter first. This curve is to scale (it is the liquid–vapour curve of section 6, zoomed in). Drag along it, or pick a place.

8 · Regelation: a wire that cuts through ice and leaves it whole

A thin metal wire hangs over a slab of ice with heavy weights on both ends. Slide time forward. The wire sinks through the ice, yet the slab never splits.

The cycle. (1) The thin wire presses hard on the ice just under it; higher pressure lowers the melting point (section 6), so that ice melts. (2) The water squeezes round to the top of the wire. (3) There the pressure is back to normal, so the water refreezes (regelation = freezing again). The latent heat released by refreezing is conducted down through the metal wire and helps melt the next layer below, which is why a metal wire works much better than a string.

NCERT uses the same idea to explain skating: the skate’s pressure melts a film of water that acts as a lubricant. simplified picture Pressure melting alone cannot explain skating on ice colder than about −3.5 °C, yet people skate on much colder ice. Friction heating by the moving blade and a thin liquid-like layer that is always present on an ice surface do most of the work.
