Heat Engines and the Second Law

An engine takes heat from something hot, turns part of it into work, and must dump the rest into something cold. On this page each flow of energy is a band whose width is the amount of energy, so the first law is just “widths add up”. The second law is about which pictures can never happen.

1 · Where the heat goes

Per cycle, the engine takes QH from the hot reservoir (scaled to 100 units), delivers W, and rejects QC to the cold one. Push the efficiency up and see how far you can go.

First law: QH = W + QC. The band that comes in is exactly as wide as the two that leave. Efficiency η = W/QH = 1 − QC/QH is the fraction of the incoming band that turns right.

Second law: the QC band can never shrink to zero. No engine between TH and TC beats a reversible (Carnot) engine, ηCarnot = 1 − TC/TH, temperatures in kelvin. Real engines lose more to friction and to heat leaking across finite temperature differences, so they sit well below the limit.

2 · Two pictures that never happen

Both of these obey the first law: energy is conserved in each. Nature still never does them. Each is one way of stating the second law.

Kelvin–Planck: no perfect engine

No process is possible whose sole result is the absorption of heat from a reservoir and the complete conversion of the heat into work. Some heat must always go to a colder body.

Clausius: no perfect refrigerator

No process is possible whose sole result is the transfer of heat from a colder object to a hotter object. Heat runs uphill only if something pays in work.

The two statements are the same law

3 · The Carnot cycle

The best possible engine: every step reversible. Two isotherms (in contact with a reservoir, taking or giving heat at a single temperature) joined by two adiabats (insulated, changing the temperature between the two reservoirs). Step through the four strokes.

Why η < 1 always: you would need TC = 0 K, and absolute zero cannot be reached. Why the cold reservoir is necessary: the adiabats alone cannot bring the gas back. To return to state 1 you must compress it, and compressing it at a lower temperature (lower pressure) is what makes the return trip cheaper than the outward one. The heat dumped at TC is the price of that cheaper return.

4 · Run it backwards: refrigerator and heat pump

Reverse every arrow. Now work goes in, heat is pulled out of the cold side and a bigger amount is dumped on the hot side. A refrigerator and a heat pump are the same machine; they differ only in which flow you are paying for.

Coefficient of performance = what you want ÷ what you pay. Refrigerator: β = QC/W (NCERT writes α). Heat pump: QH/W = β + 1. Unlike efficiency, these are usually bigger than 1: the work only moves heat, it doesn’t have to supply it.

Carnot limits: βmax = TC/(TH − TC), heat pump max TH/(TH − TC). The closer the two temperatures, the cheaper it is to pump heat. Pushing the COP past the limit (above 100%) would let you build the forbidden Clausius device.

5 · Reversible vs irreversible

A gas on a reservoir expands from 1 L to 4 L. Let it go in a few sudden steps (drop the outside pressure, let the piston jump, wait) or in many small ones. The shaded area is the work the gas actually does against the outside pressure.

A reversible process can be run backwards so that both the system and its surroundings return to where they started, with no change anywhere else. It needs the process to be quasi-static (infinitely slow, always in equilibrium) and free of friction, viscosity and other dissipation. Real processes are irreversible; reversible ones are the limiting ideal, and that is why the Carnot engine, built only from reversible steps, sets the upper limit. simplified model Each step: outside pressure drops (or rises) suddenly, the piston settles against it, and the gas returns to the reservoir temperature before the next step.
