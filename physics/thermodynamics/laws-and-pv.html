Thermodynamics: the PV Diagram

A gas in a cylinder can gain or lose energy in two ways only: heat Q flows in through the walls, and work W leaves through the moving piston. What stays behind changes its internal energy U. On a pressure–volume diagram, every state is a point, every process is a path, and the work is the area under that path. This page builds the first law from that picture.

1 · Zeroth law: what a thermometer really tells you

Two blocks, A and B. Swap the wall between them for a conducting one and heat flows from the hotter to the colder until it stops. Temperature is the property that is equal when it stops.

Zeroth law: two systems each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in thermal equilibrium with each other. The third system is your thermometer C. If C reads the same on A and on B, you know without trying that no heat will flow between A and B. That shared reading is what “same temperature” means.

Model: equal heat capacities, so both blocks meet at the average. The arrow width is proportional to the temperature difference driving the flow.

2 · Work is the area under the path

First law bookkeeping

Why the adiabat is steeper than the isotherm. Both dashed curves start at A. Expanding along the isotherm, the reservoir keeps topping up the energy the gas spends as work, so T stays put. Expanding adiabatically, nothing tops it up: the gas pays for its work out of U and cools as well, so P falls for two reasons at once. At the same point the slopes are −P/V for the isotherm and −γP/V for the adiabat, and γ = Cp/Cv > 1. Switch the gas to compare: monatomic (γ = 5/3) is steeper than diatomic (γ = 7/5).

3 · Cycles: net work is the enclosed area

Go round a loop and come back to the start. U depends only on the state, so it comes back too: ΔU = 0, and the net heat in equals the net work out. Going right (expansion) the gas does work; coming back left (compression) work is done on it. What’s left over is the area inside the loop. Drag the corners.

4 · Two paths, same start and end

From A to B in two ways. Path 1: expand at constant pressure, then cool at constant volume. Path 2: cool at constant volume first, then expand. Drag B.

Predict: which path takes in more heat?

Pick one, then the numbers appear.

U is a state function: it is fixed by where you are (for an ideal gas, by T alone), so ΔU is the same on both paths. Q and W are not properties of a state; they describe the journey. The path that spends more time at high pressure while expanding does more work, so it also needs more heat.

5 · Why Cp is bigger than Cv

Warm 1 mol by the same ΔT two ways. With the piston pinned, all the heat stays in the gas. With the piston free (constant pressure), the gas also has to lift it, and that costs extra.

Same ΔT, same ΔU = nCvΔT in both (U of an ideal gas depends on T only). At constant pressure the gas also does W = PΔV = nRΔT. So Cp = Cv + R for any ideal gas, and the bar on the right is longer by exactly the work segment. γ = Cp/Cv is the ratio of the two bar lengths.

Theoretical ideal-gas values from kinetic theory (equipartition, ½RT per mole per degree of freedom): monatomic Cv = 3R/2, γ = 5/3; rigid diatomic Cv = 5R/2, γ = 7/5. model The diatomic value ignores vibration of the molecule; when vibration is excited (at higher temperatures), Cv is larger and γ smaller.

6 · Sign conventions: physics vs chemistry

Physics and chemistry textbooks write the first law differently. The physics is the same; only the sign given to work differs. Here is one event, labelled both ways.

PhysicsChemistry
First lawΔQ = ΔU + ΔWΔU = q + w
Work symbol meanswork done by the gaswork done on the system
Gas expandsΔW = +PΔV > 0w = −pexΔV < 0
Heat inΔQ > 0q > 0
ΔUidentical: same number in both

Why physics counts work done by the gas

Thermodynamics grew out of steam engines. There the useful output is the work the gas does on the piston, and you want that to be a positive number. So Clausius wrote heat in as positive and work out as positive, which is ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW: heat supplied = rise in internal energy + work delivered. Engine efficiency W/QH then comes out positive with no fuss.

Why chemistry counts work done on the system

Chemistry watches a reacting system, not a machine. The rule is “stand inside the system”: any energy the system gains is positive, whether it arrives as heat or as work. That makes ΔU = q + w, a plain sum. This is the IUPAC recommendation. A gas that expands pushes the surroundings and loses energy, so w = −pexΔV is negative.

Translating: Wphysics = −wchemistry. Heat has the same sign in both, and so does ΔU. Before you use a formula, check which convention the book or question is using.
