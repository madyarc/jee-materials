Degrees of Freedom and Equipartition

Heat a gas and the energy has to go somewhere: into molecules moving faster, spinning faster or vibrating harder. Each independent way a molecule can hold energy is a degree of freedom, and in equilibrium each one gets the same share, ½kBT on average. Count the ways, and you can predict Cv, Cp and γ without doing an experiment. Where the moving molecules come from and why ½mv² averages to (3/2)kBT is on the kinetic theory page.

1 · Count the ways a molecule can move

Pick a molecule, then switch on one motion at a time and watch it. Every motion that stores energy as a squared term (½mv², ½Iω², ½ky²) gets a bucket, and every bucket fills to ½kBT. Try spinning N₂ or CO₂ about the x axis, which runs along the molecule.

Rotation: spins about its centre of mass

Vibration: bonds stretch and bend like springs

Every bucket holds ½kBT per molecule, so one mole holds f × ½RT: U = (f/2)RT. Warming by 1 K at constant volume adds ½R to each bucket, so Cv = (f/2)R. At constant pressure the gas also pushes the piston back, which costs one extra R per mole (why Cp − Cv = R). More buckets dilute that extra R, so γ = 1 + 2/f falls towards 1.

2 · γ of a gas mixture JEE

Mix two ideal gases at the same temperature. Every molecule keeps its own buckets, so you add up energy, not γ. Add the Cv of each gas (weighted by moles), then add R per mole for the piston work, then take the ratio.

Same rule, written for problems: Cv,mix = (n₁Cv1 + n₂Cv2)/(n₁ + n₂), Cp,mix = Cv,mix + R, γmix = Cp,mix/Cv,mix. An equivalent shortcut: (n₁ + n₂)/(γmix − 1) = n₁/(γ₁ − 1) + n₂/(γ₂ − 1).

3 · Solids: atoms on springs, C = 3R

In a solid each atom is held near its place by its neighbours, like a ball tied by springs. It can't wander off, so no translation or rotation, but it can vibrate in x, y and z. Each direction has a KE term and a PE term: 3 × 2 = 6 buckets of ½kBT, which is 3kBT per atom.

Drawn flat; the real lattice is 3-D, so z is the third spring direction.

Per atom: 3 directions × (KE + PE) = 6 × ½kBT = 3kBT. Per mole: U = 3RT, so C = 3R ≈ 24.9 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for every solid element. This is the Dulong–Petit law. (A solid hardly expands, so Cp ≈ Cv and we just write C.)

simplified model The curve is the Einstein model: a quantum picture in which each atomic spring takes energy only in fixed steps. Hot: flat at 3R, as equipartition says. Cold: the steps are too big for the available kBT, the springs stop taking energy and C falls towards zero. The temperature axis is in units of each solid's own scale (Θ), so the curve's shape is general but where room temperature falls depends on the solid.

Measured at 25 °C

Close to 3R for ordinary metals at room temperature. Lead sits slightly above: real atomic bonds are not perfect springs, and the measured value is at constant pressure. Carbon (diamond) is the classic exception, far below 3R at room temperature: its light atoms and stiff bonds make its energy steps large, so it is still “cold” in the sense of the curve above.

Why aluminium needs more heat per kilogram than copper

Dulong–Petit is per mole, i.e. per atom. An aluminium atom (27 u) is lighter than a copper atom (63.5 u), so a kilogram of aluminium holds about 2.4 times as many atoms and therefore 2.4 times as many buckets. Per gram: Al ≈ 0.90 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, Cu ≈ 0.39 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

4 · Water: about 9R

A rough estimate: treat liquid water like a solid, where every atom vibrates in 3 directions with KE and PE, so 3kBT per atom. One H₂O molecule has 3 atoms.

3 atoms × 3kBT = 9kBT  →  C ≈ 9R

5 · Why equipartition fails when cold: frozen modes

Classical physics says every motion gets ½kBT at any temperature. Experiments say otherwise: at ordinary temperatures O₂ and N₂ behave as if they don’t vibrate, and solids lose heat capacity when cold. The reason is quantum: a molecule can only take energy for spinning or vibrating in fixed steps. A mode joins in only once the typical energy available, about kBT, is comparable with its first step.

schematic, not to scale Step sizes and temperatures are illustrative; real values differ from gas to gas. The steps for rotation shrink as the moment of inertia grows, which is the quantum reason a diatomic molecule's spin about its own bond axis (tiny I) never joins in.
