Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection, Radiation

Heat always flows from hotter to colder. What differs is how it travels: passed from molecule to molecule through matter that stays put (conduction), carried along by matter that moves (convection), or sent as electromagnetic waves that need no matter at all (radiation). This page builds each one as a picture you can change, then ends with how fast a hot body cools.

Conductors and insulators

The scale is logarithmic: silver conducts about times better than air. In a metal, free electrons carry energy quickly through the lattice. In a gas, molecules are far apart and only pass energy on when they happen to collide.

Why fluffy things keep you warm. Felt and glass wool sit right next to air on the chart. They are mostly trapped air; the fibres just stop the air from moving, so it cannot carry heat away by convection. Foam, wool and feathers work the same way.

A brass tumbler feels colder than a wooden tray on a chilly day, though both are at room temperature. Brass (K = 109) conducts heat out of your hand about times faster than wood (K = 0.12), so your skin cools quickly.

3 · Two materials: series and parallel

Join two rods end to end (series) or side by side (parallel). Before you look at the graph: in series, which rod has the steeper temperature drop?

The same thing as a circuit analogy

Thermal resistance R = L/(KA). Read H = ΔT/R like Ohm’s law I = V/R: temperature difference plays the voltage, heat current plays the electric current. Long, thin, poorly conducting rods have a large R. Series resistances add (R = R₁ + R₂); in parallel the reciprocals add (1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂). This is an analogy for steady flow only; heat has no “charge” that is conserved inside a rod while it is warming up.

4 · Ice growing on a pond

On a freezing night the air above a pond is below 0 °C while the water under the ice stays at 0 °C. To freeze one more layer, the latent heat of that layer has to be conducted up through the ice already there. Drag time forward: does the ice grow at a steady rate?

The thicker the ice, the larger its thermal resistance, so heat leaves more slowly and the next layer freezes more slowly. The rate of growth is dx/dt = Kθ/(ρLx), where θ is how far the air is below 0 °C. Integrating gives x² = 2Kθt/(ρL): thickness grows as √t. Doubling the thickness takes four times as long.

Time to grow from x₁ to x₂: t = ρL(x₂² − x₁²)/(2Kθ). simplified model The top of the ice is taken to be at the air temperature, the water below already at 0 °C, and the heat needed to cool the ice itself is ignored (only latent heat counts), so the temperature in the ice is a straight line at every instant. Ice data: K = 1.6 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, L = 3.33 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹, ρ = 917 kg m⁻³.

5 · Convection: the hot fluid moves

Heat a fluid from below. The warm part expands, becomes less dense, and buoyancy pushes it up; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place. The fluid itself carries the energy. This needs a fluid (it cannot happen in a solid) and, for natural convection, gravity.

dot colour = temperature

Natural convection is driven by density differences and gravity: water in this pot, air above a heater. Forced convection uses a pump or fan to push the fluid: a home forced-air heater, a car engine’s coolant, and your blood, with the heart as the pump keeping your body at an even temperature.

Trade winds. The same idea on the scale of the planet: air at the equator is heated most, rises and moves towards the poles at height, while cooler air streams in at the surface towards the equator. The Earth’s rotation bends this circulation, so the air descends at about 30° latitude instead of at the poles and returns to the equator as the steady north-east trade winds. simplified picture

6 · Radiation: heat as light

Every body emits electromagnetic waves because of its temperature. Radiation needs no medium; it crosses empty space at the speed of light, which is how sunlight reaches the Earth and why you feel a fire on your face at once. A black body absorbs all radiation that falls on it, and (as you’ll see) it is also the best possible emitter. Heat it and watch its spectrum.

Curves computed from Planck’s law for a perfect black body. The rainbow strip marks visible light (0.38–0.75 µm).

Wien’s displacement law: the peak sits at λm = b/T, b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K. Hotter means a shorter peak wavelength, which is why heated iron goes dull red, then orange-yellow, then white.

Stefan–Boltzmann law: the area under the curve is the total power per square metre, σT⁴, with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. Double T and the area grows 16 times. That steep rise is why radiation takes over from conduction and convection at high temperatures.

Emitting and absorbing at the same time

A real surface emits only a fraction e (its emissivity, 0 to 1) of what a black body at the same T would: eσAT⁴. It also absorbs only a fraction a of the radiation falling on it; the rest is reflected (opaque body: a + r = 1). The body sends out eσAT⁴ and takes in aσATs⁴, so the net loss is H = eσA(T⁴ − Ts⁴).

Kirchhoff’s law: at a given temperature, the ratio of emissive power to absorptivity is the same for every body and equals the emissive power of a black body. So a = e: good absorbers are good emitters, and good reflectors are poor emitters. Set T = Ts: the net flow must be zero for every e (otherwise a body could heat itself from surroundings at its own temperature), which only works if the same e multiplies both arrows.

Greenhouse effect qualitative

Clothes and cooking pots. Light-coloured clothes reflect more sunlight and absorb less, so we wear them in summer and darker ones in winter. The bottoms of cooking pots are often blackened so that they absorb as much of the flame’s heat as possible.

7 · Newton’s law of cooling

A cup of tea on the table cools fast at first, then more and more slowly as it nears room temperature. For small temperature differences, the rate of cooling is proportional to how much hotter the body is than its surroundings: dT/dt = −k(T − Ts). Drag the point along the curve.

Where Newton’s law comes from (radiation only)

Stefan: σ(T⁴ − Tₛ⁴)Newton: 4σTₛ³(T − Tₛ)

Write T = Ts + ΔT. Then T⁴ − Ts⁴ = 4Ts³ΔT + 6Ts²ΔT² + …, and when ΔT ≪ Ts only the first term matters: the net radiation loss eσA(T⁴ − Ts⁴) ≈ 4eσATs³ ΔT, a straight line in ΔT. That is Newton’s law, with k = 4eσATs³/(ms) for a body of mass m and specific heat s. The straight line is the tangent to Stefan’s curve at ΔT = 0; slide the excess up to see them part.

In practice a cooling body also loses heat by convection and conduction to the air. Newton’s law describes the total well for small temperature differences, which is why it works for a cup of tea but not for a red-hot iron. approximation
