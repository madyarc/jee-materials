Kinetic Theory: Gas as Moving Molecules

A gas looks still, but it is a crowd of molecules flying in straight lines, bouncing off each other and off the walls. Pressure is the push of those bounces. Temperature measures how much kinetic energy the molecules carry. Every gas law follows from this picture, and so does the spread of molecular speeds.

1 · Pressure is molecules hitting the walls

Each time a molecule bounces off a wall, its velocity component into the wall reverses and the wall gets a push of 2mv⊥. Add up the pushes per second on each unit of wall and you have the pressure. Change temperature, number of molecules or volume, and watch the measured pressure follow the gas laws. Drag the right-hand wall to change the volume.

From bounces to P = ⅓ n m⟨v²⟩. A molecule with velocity component vx gives the wall 2mvx per hit, and the faster it moves the more often it comes back, so its contribution goes as m vx². Averaging over all molecules, P = n m⟨vx²⟩. Motion is the same in every direction, so ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨vy²⟩ = ⟨vz²⟩ = ⅓⟨v²⟩, which gives P = ⅓ n m⟨v²⟩ (n = molecules per unit volume). Together with PV = NkBT this says the mean kinetic energy per molecule is (3/2)kBT. 2D model The box is flat, so there the factor is ½ instead of ⅓ (two directions share the motion, not three); the gas laws come out the same.

Avogadro’s law. In the Avogadro experiment, record points with helium, then switch to argon and record more. They fall on the same line: at the same V and T, pressure depends only on how many molecules there are, not which. So equal volumes of gases at equal T and P hold equal numbers of molecules. One mole (NA = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules) of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at 273 K and 1 atm, and PV = µRT = NkBT with R = NAkB.

Dalton’s law. Choose the mixture. Each gas bounces off the walls on its own, so the pressure splits into a helium part and an argon part, and they add: P = P1 + P2. Each partial pressure is what that gas would exert alone in the same box. Press “All the same speed” too: argon molecules start with ten times the kinetic energy of helium, and collisions share it out until the averages match.

Brownian motion. Add a dust grain: it is far heavier than the molecules, so you can’t see what pushes it, yet it jitters in a random path. Robert Brown saw pollen grains do this in water in 1827; it was later explained as uneven bombardment by molecules, direct evidence that they exist and keep moving. The smaller the grain and the hotter the fluid, the livelier the jitter. qualitative

2 · Temperature is average kinetic energy

Three gases at the same temperature. Their molecules carry the same average translational kinetic energy, (3/2)kBT, whatever the gas. A heavy molecule therefore moves slowly, a light one fast: ½mv² is shared equally, v is not.

Mean kinetic energy per molecule, ½m⟨v²⟩ = (3/2)kBT, with kB = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K. Only T appears: not the pressure, the volume or the kind of gas. Solving for speed, vrms = √(3kBT/m) = √(3RT/M), so at the same T, vrms ∝ 1/√M. Xenon is about 33 times heavier than helium, so it is about √33 ≈ 5.7 times slower.

This (3/2)kBT is the energy of moving from place to place. Molecules with more than one atom can also rotate and vibrate; how that energy is shared out is on the degrees of freedom page.

3 · Not one speed but a spread: the three speeds beyond NCERT · JEE

Collisions keep shuffling energy, so at any moment some molecules are slow, most are middling and a few are very fast. The Maxwell speed distribution gives the fraction of molecules at each speed. The total area under the curve is 1: every molecule is somewhere under it. Step through the three “typical” speeds and see why each sits where it does.

Why rms is never smaller than the average

Five molecules with speeds on a line (any units). Drag them. The average adds speeds; rms adds squared speeds, so one fast molecule raises rms more than it raises the average.

Average = (v₁ + … + v₅)/5. rms = √((v₁² + … + v₅²)/5). They are equal only when every speed is the same; any spread makes rms larger. The Maxwell curve has a long fast tail, so vrms ends up about 8.5% above vavg.

Shape with T and M. Heat the gas (or pick a lighter one) and the whole curve stretches to the right: speeds scale as √(T/M). Because the area must stay 1, a wider curve must also be lower. 4 · Heavier is slower: diffusion and effusion JEE

Two gases at the same temperature and pressure leak through tiny holes. Molecules escape at a rate set by how fast they reach the hole, and at the same T that speed goes as 1/√M.

Graham’s law: rate₁/rate₂ = √(M₂/M₁), at the same T and P. It is the 1/√M dependence of molecular speed showing up in a bulk measurement.

5 · Mean free path: how far before the next collision

Molecules move at hundreds of metres per second, yet a smell takes many seconds to cross a room. Each molecule keeps hitting others and changing direction. Follow one: each straight stretch is a free path. Crowd the gas or fatten the molecules and the stretches get shorter.

A molecule of diameter d hits any other whose centre comes within d of its own, so it sweeps out a tube of cross-section πd². With n molecules per unit volume it meets one about every 1/(nπd²) of travel. The others are moving too, which makes collisions a little more frequent: l = 1/(√2 n π d²). Double the crowding and l halves; double d and l drops to a quarter.

6 · The assumptions, and when a real gas breaks them

The ideal gas is a model. It works when molecules are far apart compared with their size and rarely feel each other. Squeeze the gas (high pressure) or slow it down (low temperature) and that stops being true.

PV/(µRT) is exactly 1 for an ideal gas at every pressure. A real gas dips below 1 where attractions pull molecules together, and rises above 1 at high pressure where their own volume matters.
