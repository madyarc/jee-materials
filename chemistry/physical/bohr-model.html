Bohr's Atom and the Hydrogen Spectrum

Hot hydrogen gives out light at only a few sharp wavelengths. In 1913 Niels Bohr explained why. The electron may sit only on certain orbits, each with a fixed energy, and every line in the spectrum is one jump between two of them. Here you can make the jumps yourself. The second half of the page shows why Bohr's orbits had to be replaced: the electron is also a wave, and an orbit is only allowed if that wave closes on itself.

For every Bohr orbit, kinetic energy K = −E and potential energy U = 2E. JEE

Why the levels crowd together near 0. Eₙ = −RH/n², RH = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. Going from n = 1 to 2 recovers three-quarters of the whole binding energy; all the remaining levels share the last quarter. The top of the ladder, E = 0 at n = ∞, is a free electron at rest: reaching it is ionisation. Energies are negative because the bound electron has less energy than that free electron; n = 1, the most negative, is the most stable state (the ground state).

Orbit size grows as n². rₙ = 52.9 n²/Z pm: n = 2 is 4 times as wide as n = 1, n = 3 nine times. For an ion with more protons (He⁺, Li²⁺) the pull is stronger: every orbit shrinks by 1/Z, every energy deepens by Z², and the electron moves faster, vₙ = 2.19 × 10⁶ Z/n m s⁻¹. Watch all three when you change the ion.

3 · The series: every line ending on the same level

All jumps that end on n = 1 form one family of lines (the Lyman series), all that end on n = 2 another (Balmer), and so on. Pick a series. Its arrows light up on the ladder and its lines on the strip. Look at the short-wavelength end of each family: the lines crowd together toward a series limit, the jump from n = ∞.

Rydberg's formula, found from measurements before Bohr: ν̄ = 1/λ = 109 677 (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) cm⁻¹, with n₁ = the level the series ends on and n₂ = n₁ + 1, n₁ + 2, … Bohr's ΔE = RH(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) together with ΔE = hν = hc ν̄ gives exactly this formula. That match is what made people believe him.

Only the Balmer series falls in the visible band (400–750 nm). Lyman is all ultraviolet, because every jump to n = 1 is large. Paschen, Brackett and Pfund lie in the infrared, because the jumps between high levels are small. Near its limit, even the Balmer series slips into the near ultraviolet.

Each line belongs to one particular jump. A sample holds a huge number of atoms making different jumps at once, so all the lines appear together. How bright a line is depends on how many photons of that wavelength are given out (or absorbed).

4 · How many lines can come out?

A sample of hydrogen atoms is excited up to level n. On the way down, an electron can jump from any level to any lower one, in one step or several. Predict how many different lines appear for n = 4, then step n and count the arrows.

Why n(n − 1)/2: each line joins a pair of levels, and from n levels you can choose n(n − 1)/2 pairs. Each pair gives one line however the electron got there.

5 · Where Bohr's model breaks

Bohr explains the positions of hydrogen's lines very well. Look closer, or put the atom in a field, and it fails. Switch views.

Atoms with more than one electron. Even helium, with two electrons, has a spectrum Bohr's model cannot calculate: the electrons repel each other, and the model has no way to include that.

Chemical bonds. The model cannot say why atoms join to form molecules.

The deeper problem (sections 6–8): it treats the electron as a particle on a definite path. The electron is also a wave, and a definite path is forbidden by the uncertainty principle.

6 · Why only these orbits? The electron is a wave

de Broglie (1924): a moving particle has a wavelength λ = h/mv. Wrap that wave round an orbit. After one lap it must join its own start smoothly, or on the next lap it lands out of step with itself and wipes itself out. Drag the wavelength and watch the laps.

The wave closes only when a whole number of wavelengths fits: 2πr = nλ. Put in λ = h/mₑv: 2πr = nh/mₑv, so mₑvr = nh/2π. Bohr's fourth postulate was not a guess after all: it is the condition for a standing wave on a circle, like the notes allowed on a string fixed at both ends (see standing waves).

Press the button for each orbit: the electron's own wavelength always fits exactly n times. (A wave on a circle is a picture, not the full story: in 3D the wave is spread over the whole atom, as the quantum mechanical model shows.)

7 · Why don't we see a cricket ball's wave?

Every moving object has a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/mv. A wave only shows itself (by diffraction) when it meets an obstacle or gap about as small as λ. Compare an electron with a cricket ball on one ruler that spans 32 powers of ten.

The rows of atoms in a crystal are about 10⁻¹⁰ m apart, close to an electron's wavelength, so a crystal diffracts an electron beam like a grating diffracts light. This is how electron waves were first seen, and why an electron microscope can show far finer detail than a light microscope.

8 · The uncertainty principle: no definite orbit

Heisenberg (1927): you cannot know exactly where an electron is and exactly how fast it moves. Squeeze the position spread Δx and the velocity spread Δv must widen: Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π, with p = mv. Drag Δx down to the size of the atom.

An orbit is a definite path: a definite position and velocity at every moment. For an electron in an atom the uncertainty principle forbids both at once, so Bohr's sharp orbits cannot exist. Quantum mechanics replaces them by orbitals: regions where the electron is likely to be found. That is the next page, Orbitals and Electronic Configuration.

Why measuring disturbs: to locate an electron to within Δx you must hit it with light of wavelength about Δx or shorter. Such a photon has momentum h/λ, and the collision changes the electron's momentum by an unknown amount of that size.
