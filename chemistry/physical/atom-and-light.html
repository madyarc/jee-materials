Inside the Atom, and What Light Is

How do you find out what is inside something far too small to see? You shoot things at it and watch how they bend. Here you can redo the experiments that found the electron, measured its charge, found the nucleus and showed that light comes in packets. Then you will see why these results forced a new model of the atom.

1 · Cathode rays: the electron and its e/m

At low pressure and high voltage, a beam leaves the cathode (−) and flies toward the anode (+). Some of it passes through a hole in the anode and makes a spot glow on a zinc-sulphide screen. Thomson put an electric field (between two plates) and a magnetic field across its path. Switch them on and off and watch the spot.

electron beamE field (top plate +)⊗ B field into the pagePlates 3 cm long, 1 cm apart; screen 8 cm beyond (schematic)

What the beam showed: it travels in straight lines with no field; it bends like a stream of negative particles; and it is the same whatever metal the cathode is made of and whatever gas is in the tube. So the same particle, the electron, is part of every atom. A bigger charge or a stronger field means more bending; a heavier particle bends less. Only the ratio e/m can be found this way: e/mₑ = C kg⁻¹.

2 · Millikan: charge comes in whole electrons

A tiny oil drop falls between two plates. X-rays knock electrons off air molecules, and the drop picks up a few of them. Turn up the voltage: the electric pull on its charge grows until it balances the drop's weight, and the drop hovers. Then qE = mg, so the charge is q = mgd/V.

fine:

The drop's mass is found first, from how fast it falls with the plates switched off. Forces on the drop: its weight (down), the electric force on its negative charge (up, toward the + plate) and air drag, which always opposes its motion and vanishes when it stops. simplified model Speeds on screen are not to scale.

Every charge Millikan measured

Each hovering drop you record adds one mark. Or add 150 simulated drops, each measured with about ±3 % scatter.

The charges pile up at 1e, 2e, 3e … and nowhere in between. Charge is quantised: q = ne, with e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Your own drops carry a small reading error (a drop that drifts by less than about 1.5 % looks still), so they scatter a little round the lines, as real measurements do. Combining e with Thomson's e/m gives the electron's mass, mₑ = e ÷ (e/mₑ) = kg.

3 · Canal rays, the proton and the neutron

With a perforated cathode, a second beam appears behind it, going the other way: canal rays. They bend the opposite way to cathode rays, so they are positive. Change the gas in the tube and compare the two beams' e/m.

Log scale: each tick is ten times the last.

The lightest positive particle, from hydrogen, is the proton. Some ions carry two or more units of charge (He²⁺ above), but always a whole number of e.

The neutron (Chadwick, 1932): α particles hitting beryllium knock out a neutral particle slightly heavier than the proton. ⁹₄Be + ⁴₂He → ¹²₆C + ¹₀n. With no charge, it is not bent by any field, which is why it was found last.

Other rays found at the same time

X-rays (Röntgen): made when fast electrons hit a metal target; not bent by fields; very short wavelength (about 0.1 nm) and pass easily through matter. From radioactive elements: α = helium nuclei (charge +2, mass 4 u), β = fast electrons, γ = neutral, high-energy radiation like X-rays. Rough penetrating power α : β : γ ≈ 1 : 100 : 1000. schematic

4 · Plum pudding or nucleus? Fire α particles at gold

Thomson's model: a ball of positive charge, radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m, with electrons dotted through it like plums in a pudding, mass spread evenly. Rutherford fired fast α particles at a very thin gold foil. Predict: what should happen in each model? Then fire.

A foil three atoms thick, magnified. In the nuclear atom the nucleus is drawn about 10 000 times too big and its push reaches about 20 times too far, so you see far more big deflections per atom here than in the real experiment.

One α, one gold nucleus: how close it passes decides everything

JEE Advanced A head-on α stops at the distance of closest approach d, where all its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy: K = k(2e)(Ze)/d. Aimed off-centre by b, it is turned through θ with tan(θ/2) = d/(2b). Small b → big θ.

Atom: radius about 10⁻¹⁰ m. Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ m (gold's is about fm; 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m). The ratio is about 10⁵. If the nucleus were a cricket ball (radius about 3.6 cm), the atom would be about km in radius, and almost all of it empty. In the panel above, at 2 pixels per fm, the edge of the gold atom would be about m away from your screen.

Rutherford's conclusions. Most α go straight: the atom is mostly empty. A few turn sharply: a huge repulsion, so the positive charge is packed into a tiny nucleus that also holds nearly all the mass. His nuclear model: electrons move round the nucleus at high speed, held by electric attraction, like planets round the Sun.

5 · Atomic number, mass number, isotopes

The protons fix the element: atomic number Z = number of protons (= number of electrons in a neutral atom). Protons and neutrons together are nucleons; mass number A = Z + N. Build two nuclei and compare.

Try:

Isotopes: same Z, different A (different number of neutrons). Hydrogen: protium ¹H (99.985 %), deuterium ²H, tritium ³H (traces). Chemistry depends on the electrons, which follow Z, so isotopes react alike.

Isobars: same A, different Z: different elements, e.g. ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N.

Isotones JEE: same number of neutrons N, e.g. ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N and ¹⁶O all have 8.

For an ion, count the electrons separately: fewer electrons than protons → cation, more → anion. N = A − Z holds for atoms and ions alike. Electrons in the drawing sit on a ring for counting only schematic, not to scale.

6 · Why Rutherford's atom should collapse

An electron going round a circle is accelerating (its direction keeps changing). Maxwell's theory says an accelerating charge radiates light, so it loses energy. Press Play to see what classical physics predicts.

classical prediction: this does NOT happen

The classical estimate for hydrogen: the electron spirals in after about s, less than a fiftieth of a nanosecond. Real atoms last for ever. And as it spiralled, its orbit frequency would rise smoothly, so the light given off would be a continuous smear of colours, not the sharp lines atoms actually emit (section 12).

Why not let the electron stand still? Then nothing stops the attraction pulling it straight into the nucleus. The model also says nothing about how electrons are arranged or what energies they have. These failures led to Bohr's model.

7 · Light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields

An oscillating charge sends out an electric field and a magnetic field that oscillate at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. No medium is needed; in vacuum every such wave moves at the same speed c. Stretch the wave and watch the counters.

c = νλ. All waves move at the same c, so a longer wave means fewer crests passing each second. Frequency ν = crests per second (hertz, Hz = s⁻¹). Wavenumber ν̄ = 1/λ = waves per unit length; chemists quote it per centimetre (cm⁻¹).

c = 2.99792458 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ (exactly, by definition of the metre). Wave speed and λ, ν in general: Travelling Waves.

8 · The electromagnetic spectrum

Radio waves, microwaves, light, X-rays and γ-rays are all the same kind of wave. Only the wavelength differs, by a factor of more than 10¹⁶. Drag the marker along the strip (or on the visible band below it).

9 · Hot bodies and Planck's quantum

A heated iron rod glows dull red, then brighter orange, then white, and very hot bodies look bluish. An ideal absorber and emitter, a black body (best approximated by a cavity with a tiny hole), gives out a spectrum that depends only on its temperature. Classical wave theory could not explain its shape. Nor could it explain the photoelectric effect (section 10), how the heat capacity of solids changes with temperature, or the line spectra of atoms (section 12). All of these say that energy is taken in and given out in fixed amounts.

measured shape (Planck's law)classical wave theorypinned curves

The curve rises, peaks, and falls; as T goes up, the peak moves to shorter wavelengths. Classical theory (dashed) agrees at long wavelengths but shoots up without limit at short ones, predicting endless ultraviolet energy JEE: “ultraviolet catastrophe”.

Planck (1900) fixed it by assuming the oscillating atoms in the walls can give out or take in energy only in whole packets, quanta, of size E = hν, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. High-frequency packets are so large that hardly any get emitted, which pulls the curve down. More on these curves: Heat Transfer (Wien's and Stefan's laws).

10 · The photoelectric effect lab

Light falls on a clean metal plate in a vacuum, and electrons may fly off to a collector. Predict first: if dim red light ejects nothing, will very bright red light do it? Then try it.

Maximum kinetic energy against frequency

Einstein (1905): light is a stream of photons, each of energy hν. One photon gives all its energy to one electron, at once. Part pays the work function W₀ = hν₀ (the cost of escaping the metal); the rest is kinetic energy: hν = W₀ + ½mₑv².

So every metal gives a straight line of slope h, the same for all (the lines are parallel), starting at its own threshold frequency ν₀. Brighter light means more photons, so more electrons (a bigger current), but no single electron gets more energy. Electrons come out with no delay, because one photon is enough.

JEE A reverse voltage that just stops the fastest electrons, the stopping potential V₀, measures KEmax = eV₀.

Work functions depend on how clean the surface is and on the crystal face, so books quote slightly different values. These are typical measured values for clean surfaces. NCERT's table uses slightly different ones (Na 2.3 eV, K 2.25 eV, Mg 3.7 eV); use the book's values when a question gives no value.

11 · Wave or particle? Both

Interference and diffraction need waves; the black body and the photoelectric effect need particles. So which is light? Send it through slits one photon at a time and look at the screen.

beyond the syllabus Not asked in JEE, but this is what quantum mechanics really says.

Observing light as it leaves the slit: particle-like hits, a wave-like pattern

one photon's hitpredicted pattern (wave model)Slits drawn magnified; screen far away (drawn close)

Each photon lands at one point, like a particle. But where they land builds up the bright and dark bands of a wave pattern, and with two slits open the pattern is not just two single-slit patterns added up. Light travels as a wave and gives up its energy to matter (screen, metal, eye) as particles: its dual behaviour. Electrons turn out to behave the same way (de Broglie): Bohr's Model.

12 · Continuous and line spectra

A prism bends violet light most and red least, spreading white light into a rainbow with no gaps: a continuous spectrum. Hot hydrogen gas does something very different. Predict what its spectrum looks like, then switch it on.

Hydrogen's lines at their measured wavelengths; colours approximate. Visible light here is 400–750 nm; Hε (397 nm) lies just beyond the violet end, in the near ultraviolet, so it is drawn grey in the shaded strip.

Emission spectrum: excite atoms (heat, electric discharge) and record the light they give back. Gas atoms give only certain wavelengths: bright lines on black, a line or atomic spectrum.

Absorption spectrum: shine white light through the gas. Its atoms remove exactly the wavelengths they would emit, leaving dark lines: the photographic negative of the emission spectrum. (Hydrogen's visible dark lines need some atoms already excited, as in the hot outer layers of a star; cool hydrogen absorbs only its ultraviolet lines.)

Fingerprints: every element has its own unique set of lines. Bunsen identified elements this way; Rb, Cs, Tl, In, Ga and Sc were discovered by their spectra, and helium was found in the Sun before it was found on Earth.

Lines mean an atom can only lose energy in certain fixed amounts: its energy is quantised. Why hydrogen gives exactly these lines, and the full Lyman, Balmer and Paschen series: Bohr's Model of the Hydrogen Atom.
