Orbitals and Electron Configuration

Bohr gave the electron a track. Quantum mechanics replaced the track with a cloud: you can only say how likely the electron is to be found at each place. This page builds that cloud, gives every orbital its address (n, l, ml, ms), shows the shapes and their nodes, and then fills the orbitals of every element from hydrogen to krypton, including the two that break the pattern.

1 · An orbit or an orbital?

Left: Bohr’s hydrogen atom, a definite circular path. Right: what you would get if you could catch a 1s electron again and again and mark where it was each time. Press Measure and watch the picture build up.

An orbital is a wave function ψ, one of the solutions of the Schrödinger equation for the electron. ψ itself has no physical meaning. |ψ|² is the probability density: the probability per unit volume. Probability of finding the electron in a small volume = |ψ|² × that volume.

An orbit needs the exact position and the exact velocity at every instant. The uncertainty principle forbids knowing both, so a Bohr orbit can never be observed (see Bohr’s model).

Why draw a boundary at 90 % and not 100 %? |ψ|² falls off with distance but never becomes exactly zero, so no sphere of finite size holds the electron for certain. The 90 % surface is the conventional “shape” of an orbital.

2 · Four quantum numbers: an address

Each electron in an atom has an address, like house → floor → flat → bed. Shell n, then subshell l, then orbital ml, then spin ms. Step the numbers, including values that are not allowed, and see what the atom accepts.

The rules come out of solving the Schrödinger equation: n = 1, 2, 3 … (shell K, L, M, N…: size and, mostly, energy); l = 0 … n − 1 (shape: s, p, d, f, g); ml = −l … +l, so 2l + 1 orbitals in a subshell (orientation); ms = ±½, the electron’s own spin. Spin was added in 1925 (Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit) because some spectral lines turned out to be close doublets that n, l, ml could not explain. The “spinning top” picture of spin is only a picture: spin is a built-in property, like charge.

3 · Shapes and nodes

A thin slice through the nucleus. Dot density ∝ |ψ|², the colour is the sign (phase) of ψ. Pick an orbital, count its nodes (places where the dots vanish), then reveal.

ψ positiveψ negativenode

Colours are the sign of ψ, not charge: the electron cloud is all negative. The sign matters later, when orbitals overlap to form bonds.

Node counting: radial nodes = n − l − 1 (spheres), angular nodes = l (planes, or the two cones of dz²), total = n − 1. The three p orbitals are the same shape pointing along x, y and z; four d orbitals are four-leaf clovers and dz² is a dumbbell with a ring. All five 3d orbitals have the same energy in a free atom. There is no simple match between ml = −1, 0, +1 and the labels x, y, z.

Penetration. Compare 2s with 2p (or 3s, 3p, 3d; or 4s with 3d). The s curve has small inner humps close to the nucleus. An outer electron in an s orbital spends part of its time inside the inner shells, where it feels more of the nuclear charge. So s electrons are shielded less and held more tightly than p, and p more than d: s < p < d in energy for the same n. That is why 4s fills before 3d. 5 · Orbital energies: hydrogen vs everything else

With one electron, energy depends on n only: 2s and 2p are the same (degenerate). With more electrons, repulsion and shielding split each shell, and the levels can overtake those of the next shell. Step through the filling order.

rule of thumb (n + l) rule: lower n + l fills first; on a tie, lower n first. It is a guide, not a law: the real order depends on the atom, and exceptions exist (section 7).

The energy of the same orbital falls as the nuclear charge grows: E2s(H) > E2s(Li) > E2s(Na) > E2s(K). In hydrogen, 1s is the ground state; an electron in 2s, 2p or higher is in an excited state.

6 · Build the atoms, H to Kr

Three rules fill the boxes. Aufbau: lowest energy first. Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins (no two electrons share all four quantum numbers). Hund: in a set of equal-energy orbitals, spread out with parallel spins before pairing. Pick an element, or switch on “I place it” and put the next electron in yourself.

Boxes are drawn in filling order and climb with energy (schematic, not to scale). The electrons in the noble-gas core ([He], [Ne], [Ar]) are core electrons; those added in the outermost shell are valence electrons, and they decide the chemistry: why Na reacts like K, why Ne and Ar hardly react at all.

After Kr the same pattern repeats: 5s, 4d, 5p (Rb to Xe); then 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p; then 7s, 5f, 6d.

7 · Chromium and copper break the pattern

The filling order predicts Cr = [Ar] 3d⁴4s² and Cu = [Ar] 3d⁹4s². Spectroscopy finds 3d⁵4s¹ and 3d¹⁰4s¹: one 4s electron moves into 3d. 4s and 3d are so close in energy here that a small extra stability tips the balance.

Each arc joins two 3d electrons with the same spin: one possible exchange (as in NCERT’s exchange diagram).

textbook explanation Half-filled (d⁵) and full (d¹⁰) subshells are extra stable because (i) the electrons are spread symmetrically and shield each other little, (ii) there is less repulsion, and (iii) the number of same-spin pairs that can exchange places, and so the exchange energy, is largest. Exchange energy is also the reason behind Hund’s rule. A full account needs more quantum mechanics than this counting.

Lower in the table: Mo (4d⁵5s¹) and Ag (4d¹⁰5s¹) copy Cr and Cu, and Pd (4d¹⁰5s⁰) fits the full-subshell idea by giving up both 5s electrons. But W (5d⁴6s²) is not half-filled, although it sits below Cr. For these heavier atoms the levels are so close that no simple rule predicts them. Learn Cr and Cu; treat the heavier ones as data.
