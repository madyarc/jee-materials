The Unit Circle

The six trig functions are six lengths you can draw on one circle of radius 1. Drag the point round the circle (or along the graph) and watch the lengths change and unroll into the familiar waves. The identities, the signs and the allied-angle rules are all things you can read off this picture.

1 · Six lengths on one circle

Signs are read straight off P: x = cos θ, y = sin θ, and every other function is a ratio of these two, so it takes its sign from them.

  • P = (cos θ, sin θ). The vertical leg is sin θ and the horizontal leg is cos θ. The hypotenuse is the radius, 1, so Pythagoras gives sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.
  • Radians are arc length. The orange arc from (1, 0) to P has length θ, because the radius is 1 (in general ℓ = rθ, valid only in radians). Half a turn is an arc of length π, so π rad = 180°.
  • tan and sec (switch them on): extend OP until it meets the tangent line x = 1 at T. The triangle with sides 1, tan θ, OT is a scaled copy of the one with sides cos θ, sin θ, 1, so the height of T is sin θ / cos θ = tan θ and OT = 1 / cos θ = sec θ. Pythagoras in that big triangle: 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ. When P is on the left half, the line meets x = 1 behind O, which is exactly when sec θ is negative.
  • cot and cosec do the same on the tangent line y = 1: 1 + cot²θ = cosec²θ. Near θ = 90° the line OP is almost parallel to x = 1, so tan and sec shoot off to infinity: that is the vertical asymptote in the graph.
  • The graph is the circle unrolled. The bold part of each curve is what P has traced from θ = 0 so far. The dashed line carries P's height (sin θ) and T's height (tan θ) across to the graph. sin and cos repeat every 2π (one lap); tan repeats every π, because P and the opposite point give the same line OT.

2 · Allied angles are reflections and turns

Every allied angle (−θ, π − θ, π/2 + θ, …) puts a second point Q on the circle. Q is P reflected in a line or turned about O, so its legs are P's legs, sometimes swapped, sometimes with a sign flipped. Pick a transformation and drag P.

The rule behind every answer: an odd multiple of π/2 (90°, 270°) swaps x and y, so the name changes (sin ↔ cos, tan ↔ cot, sec ↔ cosec). An even multiple (180°, 360°) keeps the names. The sign is whatever Q's coordinate actually is, which you can see by taking θ small and positive.

3 · Standard values come from three triangles

Nothing to memorise: each value below is a side of a triangle with hypotenuse 1 (so the side is the sine or cosine).

30° and 60°: half an equilateral triangle

Reflect the 30° triangle (dashed). The angle at O doubles to 60° and all three sides are 1, so the triangle is equilateral and the vertical side is half of 1: sin 30° = 1/2. Pythagoras then gives the other leg: cos 30° = √3/2. Seen from the top corner (60°), the two legs swap: sin 60° = √3/2, cos 60° = 1/2.

45°: half a square

Both acute angles are 45°, so the legs are equal. With hypotenuse 1, 2·leg² = 1, so sin 45° = cos 45° = 1/√2 and tan 45° = 1.

18° and 36°: the golden triangle beyond NCERT

Apex 36°, legs 1, base x. Bisect a 72° base angle: both new triangles are isosceles, so BD = AD = x and DC = 1 − x. Triangle BCD is a smaller copy of ABC, so (1 − x)/x = x/1, i.e. x² + x − 1 = 0, x = (√5 − 1)/2. Half the base over the leg: sin 18° = (√5 − 1)/4. In triangle ABD (sides x, x, 1): cos 36° = (1/2)/x = (√5 + 1)/4.
