Equations and Inverses

Solving sin x = k means finding every place where the wave meets the horizontal line y = k. An inverse function picks one of those places, by keeping only one piece of the wave. Both ideas are one picture: a wave, a line, and a choice of piece.

1 · General solutions: where the line meets the wave

Move the line y = k (slider, or drag up and down on the graph). The bold piece of the wave is the principal branch; the one crossing on it is the principal value α. Every other crossing is a copy of α, repeated by the period and by the wave's symmetry.

2 · Inverse functions: keep one piece, then reflect

sin x takes every value in [−1, 1] infinitely often, so "the angle whose sine is 1/2" is ambiguous until we keep a single piece of the wave. Slide the window (always one half-period wide) and see which pieces work. Reflecting the kept piece in the line y = x gives the inverse graph.

Is this piece a good inverse?

Principal ranges: sin⁻¹ → [−π/2, π/2], cos⁻¹ → [0, π], tan⁻¹ → (−π/2, π/2). The other three follow the same pattern: cot⁻¹ → (0, π), sec⁻¹ → [0, π] without π/2, cosec⁻¹ → [−π/2, π/2] without 0.

3 · sin⁻¹(sin x) is not x: the sawtooth beyond NCERT

sin⁻¹(sin x) asks: which angle in the principal range has the same sine as x? Inside the range the answer is x itself. Outside, the answer folds back, giving a zig-zag that only touches the dashed line y = x on the principal range. Drag along the graph.

4 · tan⁻¹x + tan⁻¹y: when the formula needs ±π beyond NCERT

Each tan⁻¹ is an angle between −π/2 and π/2, so their sum can be anywhere in (−π, π). But tan⁻¹((x + y)/(1 − xy)) can only report an angle in (−π/2, π/2). When the true sum is too big, the formula is off by exactly π. Drag the point (x, y) to see where.
